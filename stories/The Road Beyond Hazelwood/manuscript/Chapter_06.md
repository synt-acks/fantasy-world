# Chapter Six

## The Knife in the Box

The key was still in Father’s vest pocket.

Philip had found it while checking his father for other injuries, though he had not meant to take it. He had put it back at first. Then the wagon’s burning frame shifted, and he saw that the box was still trapped beneath the broken plank.

Now he held the key in his palm and the dwarven box on his knees.

“You said Malcore,” he whispered.

Father’s only answer was a breath through his nose.

Philip could still feel the key’s shape from the first time he took it out. The small teeth had pressed into his palm. Father had kept it close, in the inner pocket of his vest, as if the lock and the promise belonged together. Philip had put it back, but now the box was half-buried under the wreckage and the promise was several days away, if they were lucky.

Philip fitted the key into the lock. It resisted at first. He turned it more gently, feeling for the place where its teeth settled. There was a small click. The lid lifted a fraction of an inch.

The box had not burned through. Its outer wood was scorched and one hinge bent, but the inside was dry. The craftsmanship had held because the joints were close and the lid fit snugly, not because the box had been made invulnerable. A good box did not need magic to be useful. It only needed someone to build it properly.

Philip ran a thumb along the edge where the lid met the base. The seam was almost too narrow to see. A line of small pins held the hinge plates in place, each one fitted so closely that the fire had blackened the wood without loosening the metal. He had seen careful work in the shop: a barrel hoop set straight, a lock with its teeth filed evenly, a scale weight balanced until it sat true. This was the same sort of care, only made by hands that knew more about metal than he did.

He wondered whether the box had been made for the dagger or whether someone had found the box and chosen it because it fit. There was no mark on the lid, no sign of ownership. The lock had no keyhole visible until he turned it toward the light.

Inside lay a dagger.

It was longer than Philip’s hand and thinner than the knives in the kitchen. The blade had a clean, bright edge, with a shallow groove running along each side. The hilt was wrapped in dark leather. Fine lines had been cut into the metal near the guard: not decoration exactly, though they had been made with care. They reminded him of the old road fitting set in the wall above the ravine.

The guard was narrow and plain. At the end of the hilt, a small metal ring had been set into the pommel. It might have been for a thong or a loop. Philip turned the dagger slowly, looking for a maker’s mark, a name, anything that would tell him who had made it and why Father had kept it locked away. The grooves ended just short of the handle. No letters he could read appeared on the blade.

Philip lifted it by the handle. It was light enough to balance, heavy enough to matter. The blade was sharp. He tested it against a loose strip of cloth, not his thumb.

The edge parted the cloth cleanly. Philip used the cut strip to replace the bloodied wrap at his hairline, then sheathed the blade before he could mistake ordinary sharpness for any other kind of power. It was a good knife. That was already enough to be careful with.

He had no wish to become a fighter. But a person could dislike knives and still need to know what one was for.

Father had called it a gift from the Slayers. He had not said what it did, or why he had carried it for years without opening the box. Philip turned the knife over. Its leather grip was worn smooth where a hand had held it many times.

He looked toward the slope. The broken road was visible above the clearing, a jagged strip of stone against the sky. The iron fitting he had noticed in the wall was gone from its place. A piece of the wall had fallen with the road and lay among the debris not far from the wagon.

Philip tucked the dagger into his belt and climbed toward it.

He did not climb far. The slope was loose, and his head throbbed whenever he moved too quickly. He took one step, tested it, then another. At the base of the fallen wall, the iron fitting was half buried in a slab of stone. The grooves he had seen from above were visible through a crack.

The fitting had been made to sit flush with the road wall. Its face was roughly the length of Philip’s forearm, dark with age, and split by a crack that crossed the narrow grooves. Rain had left a shallow puddle beneath it. Leaves and grit drifted slowly in the water, collecting at one end where the stone tilted.

Philip stopped before touching it.

There was no reason to bring the knife near the fitting. He had no reason to believe they were connected. The grooves might only be a mark left by some old craftsman. The blade might be nothing more than a good blade.

He took it from his belt anyway.

At first, nothing happened. Philip held the dagger out and turned it once in the gray light. The metal remained still. He felt foolish, and he was about to put it away when the hilt warmed beneath his fingers.

A faint vibration traveled through the blade.

Philip froze. The feeling did not come from the stone or the ground. It was small, more like a tremor in a taut string than a sound. His wrist seemed to lean toward the trees. The blade did not move on its own, but the direction felt unmistakable.

The movement was not in his hand. His fingers held the hilt steady, yet for an instant he had the impression that something had passed through the road and carried the blade’s attention with it. The feeling vanished when he drew the knife back.

He lowered it. The vibration stopped.

He raised it again, keeping it near the cracked fitting. The warmth returned, followed by the faint shiver. This time Philip felt the direction as a brief impression of movement: a path drawn through the trees, then gone before he could see where it led.

His stomach tightened. He lowered the dagger and sat on the stone. The rocks seemed to have shifted under him, though they had not. His head already hurt from the fall, and he could not tell whether this was more of the same or something new.

The woods lay below the broken road. If the knife pointed toward a path, perhaps it pointed toward the way out. Perhaps it pointed toward the river. Perhaps it was only a badly balanced blade and a tired boy’s imagination.

The sensation had seemed real. So had the wagon tipping before it fell.

Philip rubbed the hilt with his thumb. “What are you supposed to do?”

The dagger gave no answer. It was, after all, a dagger.

He considered trying again. Then he looked down the slope at Father. His breathing was shallow but steady. The light was fading, his head hurt, and the ground did not look like a safe place to practice anything he did not understand.

Philip slid the knife back into its leather sheath and returned to Father. He set the open box beside him, then changed his mind and closed it again. He had the key, but he did not lock it. The bent hinge caught against the rim.

He slipped the key into his pocket. The dagger stayed at his belt, not because he knew what to do with it, but because leaving it in the open box felt like another kind of answer he was not ready to give.

The sky darkened behind the trees. Philip pulled his coat around Father’s shoulders and sat close enough to feel each breath. He did not follow the direction the knife had shown him. He did not know what that direction meant.

The question stayed with him after the light was gone.
