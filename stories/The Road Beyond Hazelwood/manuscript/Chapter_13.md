# Chapter Thirteen

## The Giant’s Wake

They stayed on the shelf Filroy had found, keeping the stretcher in the shade of a stand of birch. Below them, the forest lay quiet around the broad scar Philip had noticed on the climb. There had been no easy moment since the crash, only moments in which nothing worse happened. Philip had started to recognize the difference.

Philip laid the stretcher down. He checked the lashings and lifted the blanket from Father’s face long enough to see that he was breathing. Father’s lips were dry. Philip poured the last spoonful of seep water into the cup and let Father drink a little. The rest he kept. It felt mean to count water by mouthfuls when Father could not ask for more, but thirst did not become less serious when ignored.

“How much?” Father murmured.

“Not much.”

“For you?”

“I had some.”

Philip hoped this was true enough. Filroy watched him without comment.

“The ground is bare along that line,” the fox said. “The animals have gone around it.”

“How far?”

“Far enough to be inconvenient.”

“That’s not a distance.”

“It is to a fox.”

Philip glanced at the corridor. It widened where it crossed the low ground and narrowed again at the foot of the hill. The forest did not look trampled by a herd. Trees lay at different angles. Some had been torn up, roots exposed; others had been pushed aside. Between them, a series of broad impressions ran in a slow line.

“Could a landslide have done that?” Philip asked.

“A landslide does not stop at a tree,” Filroy said.

The tracks were clear enough to follow with the eye, though they vanished under ferns in several places. Smaller prints crossed them, then doubled back. The animals had not all fled in the same direction. Some had circled, perhaps looking for another way to reach their dens.

Philip leaned over the stretcher. “We should move behind the rocks.”

“If we move, we may make more noise,” Filroy said.

“If we stay here, the stretcher is in the open.”

Filroy studied the rise, then glanced at the bright canvas. “The rocks, then. Slowly.”

They dragged the stretcher a few feet into the shadow of the birches. Philip set a branch beneath one pole to keep it from rolling. He checked the branch twice, then checked the slope beyond it. The hill had loose stones near the edge. If the giant came up the corridor, the stones would tell them before the trees did.

Philip stood beside the stretcher. Father’s breathing was shallow beneath the coat. The fox held still near the edge of the rise, his ears turned toward the low ground.

“It went that way,” Philip whispered.

“Something did,” Filroy said.

The ground trembled.

Philip felt the vibration through his soles. It was deeper than the earlier tremor, strong enough to make pebbles shift on the slope. The animals in the brush went silent. A bird lifted from a dead branch and flew east.

Then the giant appeared between the trees.

It moved slowly through the corridor, taller than the surrounding pines. Its head looked like a block of weathered stone, its shoulders broad beneath a rough wrap of hides and woven branches. One arm hung low. The other pushed aside a fallen trunk as if it were a reed.

The giant’s steps made the earth shudder. Each foot came down with a pause afterward, as though the creature were listening to the ground before taking the next one. It did not hurry. It did not look toward the hill.

Philip could not make himself breathe.

He had seen drawings of giants in the shop’s books. The pictures had made them seem like enormous people, only wider and more difficult to fit through a doorway. The creature in the corridor was not a person enlarged. It was a force moving through a place that had not been built for it.

The giant reached a cluster of fallen trunks. It stopped. Its head turned toward the river. For a moment Philip thought it had heard them. The wind carried smoke from the wreckage and the smell of damp leaves. Father’s stretcher lay in full view on the rise.

Philip’s hand went to the knife at his belt. He had no plan for what to do with it. The blade could cut, and its strange pull might tell him something about the road, but neither fact made it a weapon against a giant. He let the hilt go.

He looked at Filroy. The fox had flattened himself against a rock, ears pressed back, eyes fixed on the giant. Father’s face had gone tense beneath the blanket. Philip placed one hand on the stretcher rail, not to hold it in place but because he wanted to feel something that did not move on its own.

Philip reached for the blanket and drew it over the bright canvas. Filroy pressed himself against a rock. Neither of them moved.

The giant turned away.

It stepped over the trunks and continued along the corridor, angling toward a strip of exposed stone. The path ahead was drier than the ground around it. The giant followed the open stone, leaving the low, flooded places untouched.

The creature’s pace did not change. It moved as if each step were part of a decision made a long time ago and not reconsidered since. The trees broke because they stood in the way. The stones shifted because the earth could not hold the weight. Neither seemed to be the purpose of its journey.

At the edge of the corridor, a fox’s den had been cut open by a fallen root. The giant passed within a few strides of it. It did not bend toward the den or search the trees. It kept going.

Philip watched until the creature was hidden by the ridge.

Only then did he realize his knees were shaking. He sat beside the stretcher and lowered his face into his hands. His right hand throbbed beneath its wrap. He kept it closed against his chest until his breathing slowed.

Filroy crouched a few feet away, his body low to the ground. He had not taken his eyes off the corridor. “It could have reached this hill.”

“It didn’t.”

“It could have.”

“That’s why we stayed quiet.”

Filroy’s tail stayed low. He had been still throughout the giant’s passage, but now his paws shifted against the dirt as if he wanted to run and had not yet decided where.

Father stirred. Philip moved beside him and checked the blanket. “It’s gone east,” he said.

“The giant?”

“Yes.”

“Did it see you?”

“We don’t know.”

Father nodded, then closed his eyes. The answer seemed to satisfy him more than a guess would have.

“It didn’t see us,” he said.

“No,” Filroy replied.

“It didn’t come for us.”

“No.”

Philip looked toward the broken corridor. “It could still come back.”

Filroy did not answer.

They stayed on the hill until the forest began to make sounds again. A bird called once. Something small moved in the brush below. The giant’s trail remained visible through the trees, a line of crushed growth and exposed earth.

Philip made himself look at the ground around the corridor. There were no signs of a chase: no scattered carcass, no prints turning back toward the animals. The giant had not swept its gaze through the forest. It had chosen the stone and kept going.

That did not make it harmless. The branch it had moved could have crushed them as easily as the fallen trunks. But danger and intention were separate things, even when they arrived together.

Philip did not know what the creature wanted. He knew only that it had moved toward the dry stone and ignored the stretcher. Fear warned him to keep away. It did not tell him what the giant intended.

Clouds gathered behind the western ridge, dark and low. The wind changed. It carried the smell of rain before the first drop fell.

Their shelter on the hill would not last through a storm.
