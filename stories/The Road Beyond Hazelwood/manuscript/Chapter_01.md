# Chapter One

## River Rope

When seven boys came after Philip, he did not look for the biggest one. He looked for the way out.

The way out was the dock, which was not a good way out at all.

He had been trying to get home before the others caught up. Hazelwood’s school sat above the river road, and the quickest route to his father’s shop ran past the landing. Philip had taken it because it was quick. The boys had taken it because they knew he would.

He had tried the upper lane first. It passed the baker’s yard and added almost ten minutes, but the baker’s dog usually slept beside the oven door and the dog liked Philip. That afternoon the lane had been blocked by a cart unloading sacks. Philip had gone back rather than squeeze between the wheels and the wall, because he did not want to explain a torn sleeve to Father. By the time he reached the river road, the boys were already behind him.

They had not always been seven. Sometimes there were three, sometimes two. The number changed, but the rules did not: they chose a place with no teacher, found something he had said or carried, and made him answer for it until he tried to leave. Philip had learned to notice where they stood and how quickly they moved. He had not learned how to make them stop.

At school that day, one of them had taken Philip’s slate and held it above his head. Philip had reached for it once, then stopped when the others began to laugh. He had taken it back when the boy got bored. It seemed that the boys had remembered the pause more clearly than Philip had remembered the slate.

He heard their shoes on the boards behind him and kept walking. The river moved alongside the dock, brown-green under the late light. It was broad enough that the opposite bank looked like another country, though it was mostly willow trees and a stretch of pasture. A barge was tied below the far end, its ropes creaking whenever the current pulled.

“Philip.”

He stopped. There was no point pretending not to hear. The first boy stood between him and the road. The others spread out behind, making a fence of shoulders and smirks. One of them still had a piece of chalk in his hair from school. Philip had noticed it earlier and decided not to tell him. That had seemed like the sort of small kindness a person might appreciate. It had not.

“You’re in a hurry,” said the first boy.

“I’m expected at the shop.”

“Then you’d better go.”

He stepped aside. Philip moved toward the gap. A hand caught the back of his coat.

They shoved him against the rail. His shoulder struck the wood, and a burst of pain ran down his arm. Someone laughed. Philip twisted, trying to wrench free without swinging his fists. A blow caught his cheek. His eyes watered. Another struck his ribs, and the air left him in a small, humiliating sound.

There were too many of them, and they had arranged themselves so that every direction led to one of their hands. Philip tried once to push through. He was met with a shoulder and a boot against his shin. The dock boards scraped his palms.

“Stand up,” one of them said, as if Philip had chosen to sit down.

He got one knee under him. They hauled him upright. For a moment he saw the landing clearly: the mooring post, the coil of rope beside it, the dark water between the dock and the bank. Then a pair of hands struck his back.

The river closed over his head.

It was colder than he expected. Cold made the world simple. There was the pressure in his ears, the drag of his coat, the bright confusion of bubbles. He kicked for the surface and came up choking. The dock rose beside him, its boards slick and high. He reached for the edge and missed.

Above him, the boys were still there. Their faces had changed. They looked smaller now, though they had not moved. One leaned over the rail, then drew back when Philip’s hand slapped against the boards.

He tried again. His fingers found the edge. A foot came down on his knuckles.

Philip let go before the boy could stamp on them a second time. He swallowed river water and drifted away from the dock, coughing. The current carried him along the bank. It was not fast, but it was strong enough to make standing seem like a distant possibility.

He could not shout. He could barely breathe.

For a moment he kicked without choosing a direction. The water pressed his coat against him and turned the light above into a wavering patch. He forced his arms to move. One stroke, then another. He reached the surface, gulped air, and tried to keep his face above it. The dock ran past him as the current carried him along the pilings.

Then he saw a rope.

It ran from a low iron ring set into the bank, passed along the muddy edge, and trailed into the water near the landing. The current lifted its loose length and laid it down again. Philip caught it with both hands. It slipped through his fingers, rough and swollen with water.

He did not pull at once. The rope might be loose. It might be tied to something that would come away. He pressed his heels against the bank and gave the line a short, careful tug.

The iron ring held.

Philip drew himself along the rope, hand over hand, keeping his shoulder close to the bank. Mud slid under his boots. The current pushed at his legs, and the rope bit into his palms, but it led toward the shallows. At last his knee struck gravel. He crawled up until the river was behind him and his breath had become his own again.

For several seconds he stayed on his hands and knees, coughing into the grass.

The boys had gone. So had his school satchel. A book lay open in the shallows, its pages turning slowly as the river read them in a language Philip did not know. He waded in to collect it, then found the satchel downstream, where it had caught against a willow root. The leather was soaked. One strap had torn.

His cheek throbbed, his ribs hurt when he breathed, and his hand was already swelling where the foot had come down. He could have gone home by the road. Instead, he walked beside the river for a while, letting the wet sleeve of his coat hang away from his skin.

By the time he reached the square, the light had begun to slant across the shopfronts. His father’s shop stood near the center, with flour in the front window and a hand-painted board above the door. The board said GENERAL GOODS, though the shop sold so many different things that people often came in asking for something it did not have and left with three things they had not meant to buy.

The bell above the door gave a tired jingle when Philip entered.

His father looked up from a ledger. His gaze went first to Philip’s wet clothes, then to his face, then to the soaked satchel in his hand.

“You’re late.”

“Yes.”

“What happened?”

Philip put the satchel on the counter. Water ran from its bottom in a thin line. “They threw me in the river.”

His father closed the ledger. The sound was small, but the shop seemed to hear it. “Who?”

“The boys from school.”

“All of them?”

“There were seven.”

His father came around the counter. He reached toward Philip’s cheek, and Philip leaned away before he could stop himself. His father’s hand paused in the air. Then he lowered it.

“Did you hit one of them?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

Philip looked at him. “Because there were seven.”

“You can’t let them do this to you.”

“I didn’t let them.”

His father’s jaw tightened. “Next time, you fight back.”

“You know I’m not a fighter.”

“You can learn.”

“How?”

His father opened his mouth, then shut it. For a moment he looked more tired than angry. The distinction did not help Philip much.

“You start by standing your ground,” he said.

“I did. They pushed me into the river.”

“Then you get back up.”

Philip felt his bruised ribs with one careful breath. “I got out.”

“That isn’t what I mean.”

“I know.”

The words came out sharper than he intended. His father’s expression changed, not enough to name, but enough for Philip to see that he had landed a blow of his own. It was a small one. Philip had not meant to throw it.

His father glanced down at the wet satchel. “Go upstairs and change. Then bring your coat down. I’ll see if Mrs. Vale has anything to mend that strap.”

“It doesn’t need mending.”

“It will when your books fall into the street.”

That, at least, was true. His father took a clean cloth from the shelf and set it on the counter beside him.

“For your cheek,” he said.

Philip pressed it to his face. The cloth smelled faintly of soap and dried lavender. “Thank you.”

His father nodded, as if there were more to say and he had misplaced it. Philip went up the narrow stairs to the rooms above the shop. He peeled off his coat and shirt, wincing as the cloth dragged across his ribs. The bruises were beginning to show: purple along his side, red at his shoulder. He washed the river mud from his hands. The skin over his knuckles was split, though he had not punched anyone.

He stood at the basin longer than he needed to. He could hear the shop below: the scrape of a crate, his father’s voice answering a customer, the bell at the door. Ordinary sounds. The kind that had existed before the river and would continue after it. Philip found comfort in that. A shop was a place where things had labels and prices, and if a person was patient, the numbers usually came out right.

When he returned, the afternoon light lay in long bands across the shop shelves. His father was tying a parcel for a farmer. Philip took over the ledger, drying the damp cover with a cloth before he opened it. He added the day’s figures twice. They matched the first time, which was reassuring. The second time, too, which was unnecessary but satisfying.

A shepherd came in for lamp oil while Philip was totaling the flour account. He had dust on his boots and a burr caught in the wool at his cuff. He spoke to Father in a low voice. Philip kept his eyes on the ledger, though he heard the pause before the man said giant, and the way his father stopped counting coins.

The shepherd left with his oil. Philip finished the column and looked up.

“Is it true?”

His father glanced over. “What?”

“That something came down from the mountain.”

His father considered the question. He did not answer quickly, which meant he was choosing his words or deciding how many of them Philip was allowed to have.

“A shepherd saw a giant on the northern track,” he said. “It left the mountain and went into the forest.”

“How large?”

“Large enough that the shepherd did not wait to measure it.”

Philip had never seen a giant. He had seen drawings of them in old books: long arms, blunt faces, clothes made from whole curtains, as if giants had never met a tailor willing to discuss the matter. The drawings made them seem almost ordinary. A person could grow accustomed to anything on paper.

“Will it come here?”

“It’s in the forest now.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

His father rested one hand on the window frame. “No. I don’t know if it will come here.”

It was not the answer Philip wanted, but it was at least an answer. The forest began beyond the northern farms, behind the ridge. It was a place on the shop map, full of green marks and old roads. Philip had no reason to go there. People sometimes did, for hunting or timber or foolishness, but most came back with mud on their boots and less to say than they had expected.

He could stay in Hazelwood. He could help with the shop. The river and the forest could keep their own troubles.

Outside the window, the river kept moving toward the south. Philip returned to the ledger. There was still a column to finish, and columns, unlike rivers and giants, generally went where the numbers told them.
