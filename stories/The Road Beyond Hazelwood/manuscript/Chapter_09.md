# Chapter Nine

## The Fox at the Treeline

Philip made it to the first rise before he had to set the stretcher down.

The branches pressed into his palms. His right hand had swollen overnight, and the new pain from the tree climb made it difficult to close his fingers. His shoulders trembled. The slope ahead was not steep, but it was uneven, with roots and stones hidden beneath the leaf litter.

Father lay still on the canvas. Each jolt made his breath catch. Philip tried to lift the front poles without tilting the stretcher, but the ground dropped away under one foot. The frame slid sideways.

He fell to one knee.

The back end of the stretcher began to slide downhill. Philip grabbed the pole with his good hand. The canvas rasped over a root. Father’s head shifted toward the edge.

Something small struck the branch beside Philip’s wrist.

The stretcher stopped moving.

Philip looked down. A red fox stood beside the rear pole, one shoulder braced against it. The fox’s fur was thick along its back and darker at the paws. It was not large enough to carry a man, but it had wedged itself against the frame and halted the slide long enough for Philip to get his feet beneath him.

“Thank you,” he said.

The fox moved away at once, as if gratitude were a trap.

“I wasn’t helping,” it said.

Philip stared.

The fox sat on the root. “You were about to lose your father down the hill.”

Philip looked at Father, then back at the fox. “You’re talking.”

“So I’ve been told.”

“Foxes don’t talk.”

“You seem to be managing.”

Philip had several questions and no idea which one belonged first. His head still hurt. The woods smelled of damp leaves and smoke. The fox’s mouth had moved when it spoke. That was not enough to explain anything, but it was enough to make the world larger than it had been a moment ago.

He wondered if he had struck his head badly enough to hear a fox talk. Then the fox blinked at him, patient and unimpressed, which seemed like a great deal of work for a hallucination.

“What are you?” he asked.

“A fox.”

“I can see that.”

“Then we agree on something.”

Philip looked at the stretcher. “Can you help me move him?”

“I can help you keep him from sliding off a hill. I can’t carry him.”

“I didn’t ask you to carry him.”

“You were looking at my legs.”

Philip had been. He looked away. “I was trying to work out how strong you are.”

“That is usually a poor way to meet someone.”

“I’m not very good at meeting people.”

The fox watched him for a moment. “That much is clear.”

Philip let out a breath that almost became a laugh. It hurt his ribs, so he stopped. The fox’s ears turned toward the trees behind them.

“The animals are leaving this part of the forest,” it said.

“Because of the giant?”

“They leave the places it has passed through. The ground shakes. Trees fall. The smaller things know before you do.”

“Did you see it?”

“I saw what it did to my territory.” The fox’s mouth pulled back, showing small white teeth. “My den is still there. The mice are not.”

Philip glanced toward the north. He thought of the tremor beneath the berry tree and the birds fleeing the brush.

“Will it come this way?”

“Everything comes this way if the other way is blocked.”

“That sounds like something Father would say.”

“Then your father may be less foolish than he looks.”

Philip was not sure how to respond to that. He set the stretcher poles down more carefully and retied the canvas at Father’s shoulder. The fox paced nearby, not close enough to be caught and not far enough to be ignored.

“Do you know a path to the road?” Philip asked.

“There are many paths.”

“One that doesn’t go through the river.”

“That leaves fewer.”

“I need to reach Malcore.”

“You need to move your father without breaking the stretcher. Malcore is a problem for later.”

“That’s what I’m trying to do.”

“You’re trying to do it on ground that slopes toward the river.”

Philip looked at the path behind him. The fox was right. He had been watching Father’s face and the poles in his hands, not the tilt of the ground. The trail narrowed toward a wet hollow.

“Where would you go?”

“Higher.”

“Why?”

“Because I know where the ground stays dry.”

“Does the giant know that too?”

“It has been walking on the dry ground.”

“Then won’t we be following it?”

Filroy looked toward the trees where the corridor had cut through the forest. “A path can belong to more than one traveler. The giant does not own the hill because it crossed it.”

“I don’t want to get near it.”

“Then don’t follow the prints. Follow the ground that holds.”

Philip looked down at Father’s stretcher. “If you show me the higher route, I’ll decide at each part whether it’s safe enough.”

“That is what I would do.”

“You’re a fox.”

“I am aware.”

“Will you show me?”

The fox looked toward the disturbed trees. “I have reasons to know the dry ground.”

Philip waited for more. None came. The fox did not owe him a history, and Philip did not have the strength to pull one out of him.

Philip looked at him. “You’re right.”

The fox seemed surprised. “That was easy.”

“I’m too tired to argue.”

“A useful quality.”

Philip shifted his grip. “What should I call you?”

“Filroy Foxman.”

“Mr. Fox?”

“That is not my name.”

“It is what I can remember while carrying my father.”

“Then remember Filroy.”

“I’ll try, Mr. Fox.”

Filroy sighed as if the forest had burdened him with a difficult child. He walked ahead a little, stopping at a stretch of ground where the brush thinned and the earth rose above the wet lowland.

Philip followed with the stretcher. The frame caught on a root. He stopped, eased the front pole sideways, then waited while Filroy tested the ground with one paw. The fox walked around the root and pointed his nose toward a narrow rise. There was no trail there that Philip could see, only leaves and a row of stones almost hidden beneath the moss.

“That’s not marked,” Philip said.

“It is to me.”

Philip looked at the stones. He could not tell whether they were a path, the remains of an old wall, or just rocks that had ended up in a row. He did not ask the wayknife to decide. He tested the first step with his boot.

It held.

“There,” he said. “A marked trail. It stays on higher ground for a while.”

Philip looked for the mark. A pale strip of bark had been cut from a tree at shoulder height, and a second mark appeared farther along the path. It was not a sign he recognized, only a plain trail blaze.

“Does it go south?”

“It goes where it goes.”

Philip tightened the strap around Father’s shoulder and tested the knot with a tug. Then he lifted the poles again, slowly enough to feel the frame take the weight.

He waited until Filroy had moved ahead, then tried lifting the rear poles by himself. The stretcher rose an inch and dragged his shoulder down. He set it back immediately.

“You cannot do that alone,” Filroy said.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Philip looked at the fox. “I’m trying to know.”

Filroy stopped. “Then I will walk behind the stretcher on the steep places. I will not pull it for you.”

“That’s enough.”

“It is what I offered.”

“I’ll see where it goes,” he said.

Filroy turned his ears toward the forest and started along the marked trail.

The first stretch climbed between two patches of wet ground. Philip tested every step with his boot and used the branch when the trail narrowed. Filroy moved ahead, pausing where the earth changed color. He never waited so long that Philip could mistake him for a servant.

At a low stone, Philip stopped to adjust the stretcher. Father’s shoulder had slipped toward the edge again. Philip tightened the canvas and checked the knot. Filroy watched from the next rise.

“You can tell me if the ground turns bad,” Philip said.

“I will.”

“And if you don’t know?”

“I will tell you that too.”

Philip took a breath. “Good.”

The path passed a narrow stream and crossed an old line of stones. Philip looked for a marker, but the stones had no iron fittings. The wayknife stayed quiet at his belt. He did not take it out just to see whether it would answer. He could hear the stream and see the rise beyond it. For now, those were enough.

They moved another dozen paces before Philip had to stop. He set the stretcher down on level ground and waited for his arms to stop trembling. Filroy paced the ridge, alert to the forest behind them.

“You’ll need more rests,” the fox said.

“I know.”

“You are not walking quickly enough to pretend otherwise.”

Philip looked at the stretcher. “I’m not trying to walk quickly.”

“Good. The forest does not reward speed.”
