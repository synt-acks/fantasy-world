# Chapter Five

## Wreckage

Philip woke because the air hurt to breathe.

He tried to turn over. Pain lit up his shoulder and the side of his head. He stopped, waited for the bright spots behind his eyes to settle, then tried again more carefully. For a few moments he did not know where he was. He knew the smell of smoke, the taste of dirt, and the sound of something burning. The rest of the world had come apart and been put back in the wrong order.

The wagon. The road. Father’s hand on his coat.

Philip shut his eyes and tried to hold the pieces still. He could remember the crack under the wheel, the horses lunging, Father shouting. After that, there was only the feeling of falling. He could not remember hitting the ground.

The sky was a pale strip above the ridge. Smoke crossed it in slow, dirty ribbons. Somewhere below, wood cracked in a fire.

Philip pressed his fingers to his hairline. They came away wet with blood. His right hand hurt, but it moved. His left shoulder felt bruised rather than broken. He touched each of his legs in turn, then rolled onto his side and pushed himself upright.

He sat for a while with his back against the stone. When he looked down, the torn cuff of his coat was wet with blood and the front of his shirt was gray with ash. His ears rang. Each time he moved his head, the slope shifted a little. He made himself look at one thing at a time: the rock beside him, the wheel below, the strip of sky above. They stayed where they were.

The wreckage lay below the road, scattered across a shelf of rock and scrub. The wagon had broken apart against the slope. One wheel still turned slowly, though nothing was pulling it. Flames climbed the remains of the cargo bed. Smoke stung Philip’s eyes and made his head pulse in time with the fire.

The horses were gone. He could hear hoofbeats fading somewhere up the road.

“Father!”

No answer.

Philip tried to stand and nearly fell. The ground seemed to shift beneath him. He put one hand against a stone until the feeling passed, then followed the wreckage down the slope, picking his steps among broken boards and scattered crates.

He found Father below the wagon’s rear axle, half on his side, one arm beneath him. His face was pale beneath the dirt. Blood ran from a cut at his hairline and disappeared into his beard. One leg lay at an angle Philip did not like.

“Father.”

Philip knelt. He did not know what to do first. He had read that a person who fell from a height might have broken bones. He had also read that moving them could make matters worse. Books were excellent at explaining the dangers of a situation after somebody else had solved it.

He looked for a wound in Father’s chest or stomach and found none he could see through the torn clothes. The leg worried him more. Father’s boot pointed outward, and the cloth above it had darkened with blood, but there was no bone showing. Philip did not try to straighten it. He had been told that was how a person made an injury worse, and for once he did not need to ask who had told him.

He leaned close to Father’s mouth. A breath warmed his cheek. Philip waited for another. It came, shallow but steady.

“You’re alive,” he said.

He said it again under his breath. The first time had been for Father. The second was for himself.

Father did not answer.

Philip touched his shoulder. “Can you hear me?”

Nothing.

The wagon creaked. A burning beam shifted and dropped, sending sparks across the stones. Philip flinched away. Father was close enough to the wreckage that the heat had begun to dry the blood on his temple.

Philip tried to pull him by the shoulder. Father’s weight barely moved. His injured leg caught on a rock, and Philip let go at once. The sound he made was more breath than speech. He wiped his face with his sleeve and looked around for something he could use as a litter.

He searched the nearest crates. One had split open and spilled folded cloth into the dirt, but when Philip reached for it, heat drove him back. The side of the cargo bed sagged as the fire took another support. He could hear small things snapping inside the wagon: a jar, perhaps, or a bundle of kindling. He had no way to know how long the wreckage would hold together.

There was nothing unbroken nearby. No blanket. No clean boards. No spare strap. The wagon had been their shelter, their food, and nearly everything they had brought with them. Now it was a fire that kept finding new things to eat.

Philip took hold of the back of Father’s coat instead. He braced one foot against the ground and pulled, inch by inch, keeping Father’s hurt leg as straight as he could. The coat bunched under his hands. The father he was dragging seemed to weigh more than the father who had sat beside him on the wagon bench that morning.

After a few feet, Philip had to stop. His own head swam. He pressed his palm against a cut in the grass and waited until the slope held still again.

“I’m sorry,” he said, though he was not sure which part he meant.

He tried again. A little farther. Then another stop. By the time he reached a patch of bare ground beyond the smoke, his bruised hand was shaking. He eased Father onto his back, careful not to twist the leg.

The breathing continued.

Philip sat with his hand on Father’s sleeve until he felt certain the next breath would come. Then he crawled to the nearby wreckage and called for help. His voice echoed once from the ravine and disappeared. He waited. A horse answered somewhere far up the road, but there was no human voice.

He climbed toward the broken shoulder only as far as the loose ground allowed. The road edge crumbled beneath his boot. He backed away before the dirt could carry him down. From the safer patch below, he could see a length of harness caught on a rock. He tried to reach it with a branch, but the strap slid out of reach and vanished beneath a pile of boards.

When he returned to Father, the fire had shifted. A hot plank leaned toward the grass. Philip found a branch and dragged it away from Father’s coat. The wood broke at one end. He dropped it and checked Father’s leg again. It had not moved.

There was no water in any of the broken containers. Philip looked toward the river, then at the slope. He could climb down and search for a seep. He might even reach the water. But the ravine was steep, and his head had begun to spin again. If he fell, Father would have no one left to check his breathing.

Philip sat. The choice to stay had to be made again each time there was something else he could try.

Philip watched Father’s chest rise and fall. He counted ten breaths, lost count at seven, and began again. On the third try, he made it to ten. Father did not wake. Philip tucked the hem of Father’s coat beneath his injured leg to keep it from scraping the stones, then moved his hand away. He did not know whether that helped. It was the only thing he could think of that did not ask the leg to move.

Philip found a strip of cloth torn from his shirt and pressed it against his hairline. The wound was not deep, but blood had matted his hair and made the world look worse than it was. He tied the cloth around his head, clumsy with one sore hand. When he lowered his arm, his fingers came away red again.

He sat beside Father and tried to count what remained. One torn bag. Two books. The small purse of coins. No food he could find. No water. A coat. A road map torn through the middle. The horses had escaped, but the reins and harness were gone with them. The boxes had broken open on the slope, spilling nails, cloth, and pieces of the shop order into the dust.

The books were not useful as food or shelter, but Philip gathered them anyway. The history of Malcore’s bridges had lost its cover. He wiped grit from the pages and tucked it beneath the bag flap. The second book was swollen from water and smoke. He set it aside to dry, although he knew it might not.

He counted again. There was no better answer the second time.

The old road above them was broken where the shoulder had fallen. The lower road was out of sight beyond the ravine. The river made a broad, distant sound. Philip had no idea how far they were from help, only that it was farther than he could carry Father in one attempt.

He could climb back to the road. He might find the horses, or another traveler. He might find a village. Or he might spend the daylight searching while Father lay alone beside the fire.

Philip looked at Father’s face. Even unconscious, it seemed stern. The line between his brows had not gone away.

“I’m staying,” Philip said.

It was the first decision he had made since the wagon left the road. It did not make him feel brave. It made him feel tired.

The fire settled lower as the afternoon drew on. Philip watched it, keeping one eye on Father and one on the broken slope. When he thought the heat had eased, he searched the edges of the wreckage for anything that could still be useful. He found the travel bag’s torn strap, a few coins, and a tin cup bent nearly flat.

Then, beneath a charred plank, he saw a small metal handle.

Philip crouched beside it. The plank was blackened and beginning to crumble at the edges. He found a branch sturdy enough to lift it and eased the wood away a little at a time. Ash fell into the gap. The handle remained.

The dwarven box lay partly buried under the broken frame of the wagon. Its lid was scorched, one corner dented, but the lock was still shut.

Philip reached for it, then stopped. Father had said he would open it in Malcore.

The wreckage gave a dry crack behind him. Philip looked at the box, then at Father lying on the grass.

Malcore was a long way from where they were.
