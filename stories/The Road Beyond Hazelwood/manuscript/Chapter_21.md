# Chapter Twenty-One

## Aro’s Arrows

Aro made a small fire from dry twigs beneath an overhang. The flame was modest and smoky, but it warmed Philip’s hands. The city wall showed above the trees. Beyond it, a tower caught the last light and held it longer than the roofs around it.

Father lay with his head on the folded blanket. He had spoken little since they stopped. His eyes were open, though Philip could tell he was using most of his strength to remain awake.

Aro checked the arrows in his quiver. All twelve remained. Philip remembered wrapping them in brown paper at the shop. He wondered if Aro had checked each one before putting them away. It seemed likely. Aro checked everything else.

“You said your friend would have been irritated if you made too much of him,” Philip said.

Aro kept his eyes on the fletching. “He would.”

“You tell the story as if it explains what you should do.”

“Does it?”

“I don’t know. He killed an Alcrog and died anyway.”

Aro set the arrow down. “He saved me. The beast had me pinned. If he had not gone inside it, I would have died.”

He picked up the arrow again, then set it down in precisely the same place. “We had been hunting the Alcrog for two days. It had taken a mule and damaged a bridge. The village wanted it dead. We found its lair before dawn, but the wind changed while we were setting the line. I told him to wait until the wind turned back.”

“Did it?”

“No. The Alcrog came down first. It went for me because I was closest. Philip had a clear path to the rocks. He came back.”

“You told him not to?”

“I shouted it. He heard me.” Aro rubbed his thumb over a nick in the arrow’s shaft. “The story makes it sound like he had a plan. He had a knife and a moment. That was all.”

“Would you have done the same?”

“I don’t know.” Aro looked toward the dark wall. “I have spent years telling myself I would.”

“Then it mattered.”

“Yes.”

“But he didn’t survive.”

“No.”

Philip moved a small stick into the fire. “When you said I looked like a warrior, I thought you meant I should be like him.”

“I said it because I saw something familiar in you. Not the fighting. The attention. You counted those arrows twice and still knew one had a bent feather.”

“I didn’t know it was bent until you pointed it out.”

“You looked.”

“I’m not him.”

Aro leaned back against the stone. “No. You are not.”

There was no argument in his voice, but Philip had heard the hesitation before the answer. Aro’s dead friend lived in the space between what a person had done and what someone else wanted his story to mean.

“Did he want to be a Slayer?” Philip asked.

“He wanted to be useful. That is a dangerous desire. It can make a person think every risk is his to take.”

“Did he think that?”

“Sometimes.” Aro looked into the fire. “He was brave. He was also stubborn. I told the story like the first quality had made the second one wise.”

Philip glanced at the bow beside Aro. “And if the giant reaches the wall?”

Aro did not answer right away. He took an arrow from the quiver, checked its point, then put it back. “If it threatens the gate, I will shoot.”

“Even if it’s trying to get somewhere?”

“A city cannot wait for a theory.”

“It has to decide what to do before it knows everything.” Philip looked toward the wall. “But it can still wait long enough to check.”

“How long?”

“I don’t know. Until we see the next turn. Until the roadwarden sees the tracks. Until we find what is behind the farms.”

Aro’s face had gone tired. The confidence that people praised in him looked less like certainty in the firelight and more like a habit he had learned because other people needed him to choose.

“I will wait for the route evidence,” he said. “If the giant comes at the wall, I will not pretend the risk is small.”

“Would your friend have waited?” Philip asked.

Aro’s face tightened. “I don’t know. I never asked him what he would do in a different hunt. I only remember what he did in that one.”

“That sounds like a hard thing to remember.”

“It is.”

Philip looked into the fire. “I don’t want you to shoot because you think I’m supposed to be like him.”

“I won’t.” Aro’s answer came without delay. “Your name has nothing to do with this.”

“I’m not asking you to.”

“Good.” Aro took a piece of bread from his pack and broke it into three portions. He handed one to Philip, one to Father, and kept the smallest for himself. “Then we agree on more than we did this morning.”

Father held his portion but did not eat it at once. “You and I used to argue about which way to turn a creature.”

Aro’s eyes remained on the fire. “We argued about whether the road could keep danger away from a settlement.”

“We used a marker to pull a beast toward an abandoned quarry,” Father said. “The quarry was empty. We believed it was safe.”

“It was,” Aro said.

“Until the beast changed direction.”

“We moved the people in time.”

“Because someone saw it change direction.” Father’s hand tightened around the bread. “The people who lived there did not know we had sent it toward them.”

Aro did not look at him. “We were trying to keep the danger away from the city.”

“We were making a choice for people who had not been asked.”

Philip looked from one man to the other. “Did anyone get hurt?”

“Not that time,” Father said.

“That time,” Aro repeated, softly.

Neither of them added more. Philip understood the argument now. The road could help people move away from danger, or help someone else decide where the danger should go. The marker could not tell the difference.

Philip ate slowly. It was stale and dry. It was also the first proper food he had seen since Hazelwood. His stomach tightened around it, then accepted the next bite.

Father opened his eyes. “Philip.”

“I’m here.”

“You don’t have to prove anything to Aro.”

“I know.”

“Or to me.”

Philip looked at him. The words were careful, as if Father had to pass them through a narrow doorway. “I know that too.”

Father closed his eyes again. Philip could not tell whether he meant it, or whether he was trying to learn how to mean it.

The night grew colder. Aro took the first watch. Philip slept in fits, waking to the fire’s collapse and to the distant sound of something moving across the ridge. Each time the ground trembled, Filroy raised his head. The fox did not leave the hollow.

At first light, Malcore’s warning bell rang once from behind the wall.

A second bell answered from farther east.

Aro was already on his feet, bow in hand. “It’s close.”

Philip sat up too quickly. The world swayed, then settled. He pressed his palm to the ground until the dizziness passed.

The bell rang a third time. From the east road, where the outer farms stood between the city and the old causeway, a low vibration moved under the soil.

They had reached the city on the same morning as the giant.
