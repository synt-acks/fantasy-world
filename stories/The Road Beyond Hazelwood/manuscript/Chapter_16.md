# Chapter Sixteen

## Under the Rooted Bridge

The storm made the bridge sound alive.

Water ran through its joints and drummed on its stones. Wind pushed at the old masonry, though the bridge had endured weather before Philip’s grandparents had been born. It had been built by people who expected roads to matter forever. The people who had stopped repairing it had probably expected something else to last forever instead.

Philip called Father’s name again. The rain took it apart before it reached the far side of the abutment.

He lowered himself to his stomach and peered over the broken bank. Mud slid beneath his elbows. Below, the stream had swollen into a brown rush, carrying twigs and a piece of fence rail. He could see the stretcher’s canvas caught behind the roots, but the bridge stones hid most of it.

“Father!”

“He cannot hear you over the water,” Filroy said.

Philip turned. The fox had appeared from beneath the bridge, fur slick and dark with rain. A cut crossed one ear. He looked from Philip to the stream, then along the bridge’s underside.

“You found somewhere dry?”

“I found somewhere less wet. It is not the same thing.” Filroy shook water from his whiskers. “The stretcher is below us. The roots hold it. We need to go around.”

Philip looked toward the south road. It sloped away between trees, faintly visible through sheets of rain. They could go downhill, reach the road, and search from the stream’s lower bank. It was the easier path. It was also the way that led away from the stretcher.

He tested the harness line at the iron ring. It remained taut. The stone around the ring had cracked, but the roots had taken some of the strain. He could not see the line’s lower end.

“Can you get under the bridge?”

“I already did.”

“Can you show me?”

Filroy’s ears tipped forward. “That is a different question.”

Philip nodded. “Then show me.”

He left the wayknife sheathed. The path Filroy chose ran behind the bridge’s upper stones, away from the soft lip of the bank. Philip moved slowly, feeling each rise and fall through his knees. The rain had loosened the soil, and a single careless step could put him where the stream was strongest.

They circled the abutment on the uphill side, where the earth still held beneath a tangle of roots. Filroy moved ahead, choosing a narrow path between stones. Philip followed on hands and knees. Twice he stopped to test the ground with a branch before putting his weight on it. The branch found one pocket of mud deep enough to swallow his arm to the elbow. He withdrew it slowly, unwilling to discover whether his shoulder could be swallowed next.

At the far end of the abutment, the roots formed a low arch above a shelf of stone. The stretcher lay beneath it, tilted but caught between two thick roots. Father was still tied in. His face had turned toward the stone, and his coat was soaked through at the shoulders.

“Father.” Philip crawled to him. “Can you hear me?”

Father’s eyes opened. They moved without settling.

“Where—”

“Under the bridge. You’re caught against the roots. Don’t try to sit up.”

Father tried anyway. Pain tightened his face; one hand clutched the canvas.

“Stop,” Philip said. “The stretcher is held. I’m here.”

The words came out too loudly. Philip lowered his voice. “I’m here.”

Father blinked at him. “Philip?”

“Yes.”

“The wagon?”

“Gone.”

“Horses?”

“Gone too.”

Father shut his eyes. Philip could not tell whether the answers had reached him or only passed close. He felt for Father’s pulse at the wrist. It was fast but there. He checked the cut at the hairline. The rain had washed away most of the blood, and the wound was still open. Father’s breathing was shallow, uneven, but no longer lost in the storm.

“His leg is worse,” Philip said.

“The bank shifted the stretcher,” Filroy answered. “The lower end struck the stone.”

Philip pushed wet canvas aside. Father’s trouser leg was torn below the knee. The lower leg had swollen badly; the shape beneath the skin looked wrong. Philip did not try to straighten it. He had seen a broken bone once, years ago, when a carter had fallen from a wagon. It had looked wrong then too, and the healer had told everyone to stop touching it.

“I need to get him off the stretcher without moving that leg.”

The stretcher’s front pole had split lengthwise. The canvas held, but one corner had slipped below the root arch. Philip could see the harness line running past the broken bank and disappearing into rain. He did not know whether the line held the frame or only kept it from being swept away. He pressed a hand against the stone and listened to the water. The stream had risen almost to the lower shelf.

“We need him moved before the water gets higher,” he said.

“The water has been rising,” Filroy said. “It may rise more.”

Philip looked at the stretcher. “Can you get around behind it?”

“I can.”

“Can you push the frame toward me?”

“A little.”

“I’ll take the other end.”

“Then do not pull until I tell you.”

“You need more than two hands,” Filroy said.

Philip looked at the fox. “I know.”

It was the first time he had said it without trying to make the words smaller.

They waited until the worst of the rain passed. Filroy found a section of canvas that could be folded beneath Father’s shoulders. Philip freed the stretcher lashings one at a time, keeping the injured leg supported by the frame. He had no proper splint, only two straight pieces of wagon slat and strips of cloth. He laid the slats along either side of the leg, padded them with folded canvas, then tied the cloth above and below the swollen part. He left enough slack that Father’s foot did not change color. The knots were not pretty, but they held.

“Too tight?” he asked.

Father did not answer.

“I think he is asleep,” Filroy said.

Philip stopped twice to check Father’s foot and once to rest his hand. The cloth strips pulled awkwardly against his bruised knuckles. He used the heel of his palm wherever he could and tightened each knot a little at a time. When he finished, he traced the edge of the splint with two fingers, making sure it did not press into the swelling.

“He was awake a moment ago.”

“People do that when they are hurt.”

Philip checked the cloth again. “I’ll loosen it if it swells more.”

“A sound plan.”

They shifted Father beneath the root arch, onto a dry patch above the stream’s rising edge. Philip folded the coat around him and tucked the surviving blanket close. The wind could still reach them, but the stones blocked most of the rain. He spread the canvas over the gap between the roots and a fallen beam, making a low roof that dripped steadily at one corner.

The work emptied his arms. When it was done, Philip sat with his back against the stone and could not immediately make himself stand again. His head throbbed where it had struck the slope. His hand pulsed beneath its wrap.

Filroy sat across from him, grooming one wet paw. “You did not follow the road.”

“I couldn’t leave him.”

“That is not the same as being unable to.”

Philip watched the stream slip beneath the bridge. “I thought about it.”

“Naturally.”

“I thought if I found help, I could come back.”

“Perhaps. The river is rising. Perhaps not.” Filroy licked the rain from his paw. “Thinking of the easier path does not mean you chose it.”

Philip let his head rest against the stone. For a while the only conversation was the weather passing over them. He slept in short stretches, waking each time Father shifted or the bridge groaned.

Near dawn, Father opened his eyes and kept them open.

For a while Father only watched the water below the arch. Philip was half asleep, but the change in his breathing woke him. Father’s eyes tracked the stream, then the roots above them, then Philip’s face. Recognition came slowly.

“You came back,” Father said.

“I never left.”

Father closed his eyes. “Good.”

“How long?” he asked.

“Since the storm?” Philip leaned close. “Most of the night.”

Father looked at the splint. “You did that?”

“I used what I had. It might need a healer.”

“It does.” Father swallowed. His voice was clearer, though he still sounded exhausted. “We need Malcore.”

“We’re going there.”

“The Road Office. Aro. The markers.”

Philip held his gaze. “You’re going to tell me why.”

Father’s eyes closed once, then opened again. “Yes.”

The bridge dripped into the stream. Morning had begun beyond the roots, but Philip stayed close enough to hear.
