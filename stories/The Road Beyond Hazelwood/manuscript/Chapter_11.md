# Chapter Eleven

## Half Awake

Father woke in pieces.

First his eyes opened, then shut. A while later, his fingers found the edge of the blanket. When Philip spoke, Father turned his head toward the sound but did not seem to know where it came from.

They had reached a dry rise beyond the flooded hollow. Philip had stopped there because Father’s breathing had grown worse and his own legs would no longer keep the stretcher moving. Filroy had found a patch of ground where the brush broke the wind. The fox watched from a few steps away, pretending not to watch.

“Don’t move,” Philip said.

Father frowned. “Where are we?”

“South of the crash. On a dry rise.”

“The road?”

“The road went into the swamp.”

Father tried to lift his shoulders. His face tightened, and he sank back against the folded coat.

“Stay still,” Philip said again. “Your leg is hurt.”

“I know.”

“You knew before you tried to move it?”

“I know now.”

Philip sat back on his heels. He wanted to be angry. He was angry. But Father’s face was gray under the dirt, and there was a tremor in his voice that had not been there before.

“The wayknife led us to the flooded crossing,” Philip said.

Father’s eyes shifted toward the dagger at Philip’s belt. “You used it?”

“I brought it near the marker. It showed a path through the water.”

“It showed you traffic.”

“I didn’t know that.”

Father closed his eyes. “I should have told you.”

Philip stared at him. “You gave me a knife I didn’t understand, then let me try to use it while you were unconscious.”

“I thought I’d explain it in Malcore.”

“You said that about the box. You said that about the trip. You keep deciding when I’m allowed to know things.”

Father’s breathing rasped. “You’re fifteen. You’re hurt. I was trying to keep you safe.”

“Being kept ignorant isn’t the same as being kept safe.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Father’s eyes opened again. He looked directly at Philip this time. “I know it now.”

The words did not make the anger disappear. They did make it harder to keep using it as a wall.

Philip pointed to the blade. “What does it do?”

“It’s a wayknife. It reads a worked marker. The stone holds traces of repeated passage, and the knife lets you feel the strongest line.”

“The strongest line?”

“The route most used. Or the route that left the greatest impression.”

“How close do you have to be?”

“Within reach of the marker. It has to be worked stone or iron, and there has to be enough of it left to hold the trace.”

“So not just any road.”

“No. The knife can’t find a path through the forest.” Father blinked slowly. “It reads the marker. The rest is yours.”

“That was the heavy steps.”

“Probably.”

“And it doesn’t know if the route is safe?”

“No.”

“Does it know where the route ends?”

“No.”

“Then it doesn’t tell you much.”

Father almost smiled. “It tells you one thing. You have to find out what that thing means.”

Philip thought of the river rope. It had led somewhere because it was attached to the bank. He had checked the iron ring before trusting it. The marker had given him direction, but he had treated that direction as a decision.

“Why give it to me if you weren’t going to explain it?”

“I planned to.”

“When?”

“In Malcore. When we weren’t trying to keep you alive on a hillside.”

“You knew we might have trouble.”

Father’s gaze left him. “I knew there was danger on the road.”

“And you didn’t tell me.”

“I wanted to.”

“You didn’t.”

For a few seconds neither of them spoke. The forest moved around them in small sounds: water slipping through reeds, a bird calling once and then falling silent, Filroy’s claws on a stone.

“I thought if I told you about the Slayers, you’d think I wanted you to become one,” Father said. “I thought the knife would feel like a claim.”

“It feels like a claim when you give me something and won’t tell me what it is.”

“I see that.”

“You keep telling me to fight. Then you give me a tool I don’t understand and expect me to choose the right path.”

“I didn’t expect you to use it yet.”

“Then why did you bring it?”

Father did not answer. His eyes had begun to close again.

Philip leaned closer. “I need you to tell me what you know.”

“I will.”

“Now.”

“Not all of it.” Father swallowed. “I can tell you how the knife works. I can’t tell you everything about the Slayers while I can barely keep my eyes open.”

Philip looked at the wayknife. “That’s still more than you told me before.”

“Yes.”

Father shifted his hand, then winced. “The marker can be damaged. The reading can be wrong or incomplete. The hilt warms. You feel a direction and a rhythm. Then you stop and look around. If the ground disagrees, believe the ground.”

“How do you know which part is old and which part is new?” Philip asked.

“You learn the feel of a trace. Regular steps, carts, a crowd, a heavier thing. You compare what the knife tells you with what you can see.”

“You could tell the giant’s steps from a cart?”

“I could tell they were heavy and uneven. I couldn’t tell what the giant wanted.”

“How many times can you read it?”

Father’s eyes closed, then opened again. “Once, properly. Twice if you’re careful. More than that and you stop knowing whether the marker is moving or you are.”

Philip glanced at the flooded crossing. “I read it.”

“Then stop.”

“I already did.”

“Good.” Father’s voice weakened. “A reader learns to stop before he needs to.”

“And if the ground is under water?”

“Then don’t step into it.”

Filroy made a small sound that might have been agreement.

Philip looked at the fox. “You could have said that.”

“I did,” Filroy said. “You were busy asking the knife.”

Father’s mouth twitched. “He’s right.”

Philip looked at him, waiting to see whether Father would add a reason or turn the admission into another instruction. Father only let out a slow breath.

“I should have told you before you had to use it.”

“Yes.”

“I thought the trip would be easier.”

“Because the knife was in the box?”

“Because the road was familiar.”

“It isn’t anymore.”

Father looked toward the swamp. “No.”

Philip wanted to ask what else was in the old road. He also wanted Father to stay awake long enough to answer. He chose the question that might help them travel.

“What should I do if a marker gives me two directions?”

“Step away. Wait until you can stand straight. Then look at the land. Ask what the road is doing. Don’t ask the knife to choose for you.”

“And if I can’t tell?”

“Ask someone else.”

Filroy lifted his head. “That is a reasonable answer.”

“That’s twice today,” Philip said. “The forest is becoming difficult.”

The brief smile left Father’s face. “I’m sorry I didn’t explain.”

Philip did not answer at once. The apology was real. So was the hurt. Neither one made the other disappear.

“I need you to help me,” Philip said. “You know the roads. I don’t.”

“I can tell you what I remember.”

“And I need to tell you what I see. Even if it doesn’t match what you remember.”

Father looked at him for a long moment. “All right.”

It was not a grand agreement. It was a tired man on a stretcher giving his son one word. Philip decided to take it for what it was.

Father’s eyes closed. This time Philip did not try to wake him.

Filroy was already watching the slope below. “The wet path is not the only way through.”

Philip picked up the branch he had used to test the mud. “Show me the other one.”

“I’ll show you the beginning. You’ll have to test each step.”

Philip checked the stretcher knots, then set the wayknife back in its sheath. “At first light.”

“The ground will still be there.”

“That’s what I’m hoping.”

He settled beside Father and kept watch until the dark thinned. He did not sleep much, but he rested his legs and kept one hand near the stretcher. When the first light reached the swamp, he and Filroy started toward the higher ground.
