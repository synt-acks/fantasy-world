# Chapter Twenty

## The Old Causeway

They left the ridge after the giant’s steps had faded.

They waited another hour before moving. Philip counted the time by the light creeping across the basalt and by Father’s short periods of wakefulness. Twice Father asked where they were. Once he asked for the wagon. Philip answered both questions without correcting him sharply. The healer in Malcore would know more than any of them, but the city was still a distance away.

While they waited, Aro marked their position on the damaged map. His pencil broke through the paper where the map had been wet. He sharpened it with his knife and tried again.

“You said the old road passed behind farms,” Philip said. “How did the farms get there?”

“The city grew,” Father replied. “The road was no longer maintained. People built close to the wall. The land became useful for crops and storage.”

“Did nobody notice the turn disappeared?”

“They noticed the road had stopped being used. That is not the same as noticing where it went.”

Philip looked east, where the trees crowded together above the slope. A road could disappear by being covered, fenced, or simply forgotten by people who had never needed it. None of those things required anyone to decide to erase it.

The track ran east along the basalt rise. Philip and Aro carried the stretcher while Filroy moved ahead, checking the path. Father stayed conscious for most of the descent, though he spoke only when the ground forced them to stop. The rain had eased. Water dripped from branches and gathered in shallow pools along the old stones.

At the first marker, Father asked them to set the stretcher down.

It was a narrow slab wedged between two rocks, its face worn almost smooth. A strip of iron had been sunk into its side. The iron was cracked near the top, leaving a dark seam that ran down to the stone’s base.

“I remember this,” Father said. “There used to be a cut here. The road divided: west toward the river, east along the rise.”

“Which way did the old road go?” Philip asked.

“Both, at different points. A road can be more than one line.”

“The map you had only showed one.”

“It was a poor map.”

“Why keep it?”

“It was the one I had.”

Philip considered this. People often kept bad things because they were the things available. There were worse reasons for a map, though not many.

He took the wayknife from his belt. The stone’s iron band was intact enough to answer. He crouched close and held the hilt within a hand’s width of the marker.

Warmth spread into his palm. A pulse moved through the blade, faint but steady. He felt two directions at once: one heavy and recent, angling down toward the river; another lighter, a repeated rhythm running east along the rise. The impressions did not become pictures. They were only the sense of feet and wheels passing over ground, one after another, the older rhythm nearly lost beneath the newer one.

Philip pulled the knife away.

The eastward trace lingered in his balance. His head tilted as if the world had shifted a finger’s width. He planted his hand on the marker and waited for the sensation to pass.

“What did you find?” Aro asked.

“Two lines. The stronger one goes down toward the river. The lighter one goes east.”

“The giant’s track is stronger.”

“I think so. It feels heavier. The other one is more regular.”

Father nodded. “Carts and people. The old causeway.”

“How old?”

“Old enough that the road office stopped maintaining it.”

Filroy sniffed the ground. “The east ground stays dry past the next rise. I used it before the trees fell.”

Philip looked from one to the other. The marker offered a direction. Father offered a memory. Filroy offered the ground beneath their feet. None of those answers was enough alone, but together they seemed to point toward the same place.

They continued to the next marker. It had fallen face-down in the leaf litter. Father recognized the iron fitting, but its stone had broken into three pieces. Philip did not test it. The damage made the reading uncertain, and he had already paid for one reading with a spinning head.

At the third marker, the old road crossed a shallow wash. The post stood upright, its iron band rusted but unbroken. Aro offered Philip the knife. Philip’s balance still felt a little wrong from the first reading.

“I want to compare it,” Philip said. “But I don’t trust my head enough to know what I’m feeling.”

“A good reason to wait,” Aro said.

Father pointed toward the wash. “Markers do not lie. People ask them the wrong question.”

“Then I need to ask the ground first.” Philip studied the broad prints pressed into the mud below the post. They led toward the wash, where the bank had slumped under the weight, but there was no matching print on the far side. Higher up, old wheel ruts ran along the embankment and disappeared east beneath the thorn. “The giant went down toward the water. The old road stays on the rise.”

“The bank is too soft to cross,” Filroy said.

“And the smaller trace could be old carts,” Philip said. “Or something else.”

“The causeway,” Father said. “It used to run east behind the farms. Before the fields were fenced.”

They followed the higher ground. In places, stone blocks showed under moss. The road had not vanished so much as been covered by everything that had grown after people stopped tending it. A low embankment rose above the wash and curved east, then disappeared beneath thorn and sapling.

From the top, they could see the outer farms of Malcore. Smoke rose from the city beyond them, and the wall stood gray beneath the cloud. The old road led behind a line of fields toward a gate or barrier hidden by trees.

The giant’s tracks crossed the lower approach and continued south. At the embankment, the broad prints turned. One pressed deep beside a stone post. Another angled along the rise, toward the overgrown fields.

“It tried to turn here,” Philip said.

“Or it stepped around the mud,” Aro answered.

“That’s possible.” Philip looked at the slope, the water below, and the exposed rock ahead. “We need to see what’s past the farms.”

Father lifted his head. “If the old turn is there, it may lead to Stillstone Hollow.”

“May,” Philip said.

Father met his eyes. “May.”

The route narrowed before the camp. Aro and Philip lifted the stretcher over a fallen stone while Filroy found a way around the bramble. Father woke when they lowered him. He asked if the road had turned east.

“We think it did,” Philip said. “The ground and the traces agree so far.”

“You think?”

“We haven’t seen where it goes yet.”

Father watched him for a moment, then let his head rest against the blanket. “Good.”

Philip had expected his father to want a stronger answer. The fact that Father did not ask him to pretend felt like a small change.

They could not cross the bramble with the stretcher before dark. Aro chose a sheltered hollow on the south side of the embankment, with a clear view of the city and the track. They would rest there, then reach Malcore at first light. Philip put the wayknife away. The eastward trace was not a promise. But for the first time, the old road was more than a line on a torn map.
