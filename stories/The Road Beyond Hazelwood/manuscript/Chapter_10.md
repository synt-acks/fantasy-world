# Chapter Ten

## A Road with No Road

The marked trail kept to higher ground until it did not.

For a while Philip could follow it by the pale cuts on the trees. The stretcher dragged over roots and stones, and every few steps he had to stop to shift his grip. Filroy moved ahead, vanished behind brush, then reappeared on the other side of a fallen branch.

“This path is older than the marks,” Philip said.

“Most paths are.”

“I meant the trail blazes.”

“I know what you meant.”

Philip tried not to look at Father’s face each time the stretcher jolted. He had wrapped the canvas twice around the poles, but there was no way to keep the frame smooth over uneven ground. Father’s leg lay straight, his boot still pointed outward. His breathing caught now and then, then settled again.

The trail descended into a shallow hollow. Water stood among the reeds, reflecting a narrow strip of sky. On the far side, Philip saw a line of flat stones leading toward the trees. Some were missing. The rest sank into mud.

“We should go around,” Filroy said.

“Around where?”

“Up.”

Philip looked at the rise. It was steep and covered in wet roots. “The stretcher won’t go up there.”

“It won’t go through the water either.”

“I can test it.”

Filroy’s ears turned toward him. “You can test your own boot. You cannot test the ground for the weight of two people and a wooden frame.”

Philip glanced at the stones. There was an old iron marker set beside the first one, its top level with his hip. The face was worn but intact, with a narrow groove running down its center. The trail seemed to continue past it, though the mud had hidden most of the way.

“I’ll see if this tells us anything,” he said.

“The fox has just told you something.”

“I heard him.”

“Then why ask the knife?”

Philip had no good answer. The wayknife had pointed toward the trees at the other marker. If it could show the road, perhaps it could settle this. He took it from his belt and moved close to the iron fitting.

The hilt warmed.

The vibration came at once, stronger than before. It ran up the blade into his wrist, then spread across his fingers. Philip felt the direction as a pulse of movement: heavy steps crossing the stones and continuing through the hollow. The rhythm was slow and uneven. The ground in his mind seemed to give way beneath each step.

He pulled back, but the impression stayed for another moment. His stomach lurched. The trees leaned sideways, though he knew they had not moved.

“Philip?” Filroy said.

Philip blinked. The fox was standing on higher ground. Father lay still on the stretcher.

“It points across,” Philip said.

“What does?”

“The knife. The road.”

“It points to where people have gone.”

Philip tightened his hand around the hilt. “That’s a road.”

Filroy looked at the submerged stones. “It was.”

Philip studied the marker. The path continued across the hollow in a clear direction. He felt a faint dizziness, but it seemed to be fading. The heavy steps had gone that way. The old road had gone that way. It was the best answer he had.

He put the knife away.

“We’ll cross at the stones,” he said.

Filroy did not argue. He moved to the edge and sniffed the air, then stepped onto a patch of grass that held his weight. Philip took the stretcher’s front poles. The first stones were firm beneath his boots. The water reached his ankles, cold through the leather. He placed each foot carefully, pausing at the gaps where mud covered the path.

Halfway across, the rear of the stretcher dropped.

The canvas pulled tight. Father’s shoulder slid toward the edge. Philip turned, reaching to steady him, and the water reached his knees. The ground beneath his left foot gave way. He caught the nearest stone with his free hand.

“Don’t pull!” Filroy called.

Philip stopped. The stretcher shifted again, but the canvas held. The stone under his hand was slick. He looked at the path behind him. The water was cloudy now, stirred by his boots. He could not see the bottom.

“The marker says—”

“The marker says nothing about your weight.”

Philip swallowed. He had thought the reading would lead to a route. It had led him to a road that no longer existed in any useful sense.

He backed toward the nearest firm stone, moving one foot at a time. The stretcher caught on a submerged rock. He lifted, then lowered it carefully. Father groaned when the frame jolted. Philip stopped and waited, his arms shaking.

They reached the bank by inches. Filroy kept to the higher grass and watched Philip test each step. When the stretcher was safely on dry ground again, Philip sat down hard. The wayknife’s vibration had stopped, but his stomach still rolled.

He kept hold of the front pole even after the stretcher stopped. His fingers had locked around the wood. When he tried to open them, his right hand did not cooperate. He eased each finger loose with the other hand and looked at the water. A dark line of silt moved away from where they had crossed. The stones were still there beneath it, but the crossing had ceased to be a path as soon as they needed it.

“Father?” he said.

Father’s eyes were closed. The stretcher had shifted against the bank, but the lashings still held. Philip checked the injured leg without touching it, then the blanket, then Father’s face. He was breathing. Philip repeated the checks because the first attempt had been too fast to count as certainty.

Filroy walked the bank and found a narrow strip of ground behind a clump of alder. It was too low for the stretcher to pass, but above the waterline. He returned and sniffed the mud on Philip’s boot.

“The stones slope down on the far side,” he said. “There is no dry exit.”

“The marker showed the road crossing.”

“It showed that something crossed it.”

Philip looked down at the wayknife. His first reading had seemed like a path laid out for him. It had not shown him the mud, the depth, or the way the stones ended under the far bank. He had supplied those answers himself.

“I should have checked the far side before we moved him.”

“Yes.”

Philip did not argue. His mistake had made Father hurt more, though he did not know by how much. He could not undo the crossing. He could keep from making the same one again.

He sheathed the knife and fastened it at his belt again. Then he took the branch and tested the ground beside the water, looking for a route that did not depend on the submerged stones.

“I thought it meant safe,” he said.

“It meant traveled.”

Philip looked at the marker across the water. “How do you know?”

“I don’t. I know the ground.”

Philip turned the dagger in its sheath until the hilt no longer pressed against his belt. “I need to look at the ground.”

“Yes.”

“And I need you to tell me what you see.”

Filroy’s tail moved once. “That is better.”

Philip reached for the stretcher poles again. Father made a small sound, almost a groan. His eyelids fluttered.

“Father?”

His eyelids did not lift, but his hand moved against the canvas, and the sound that came from him was unmistakably a word.

“Philip.”
