# Chapter Eight

## A Stretcher and a Choice

Philip spent the first hour of the morning trying to move Father without moving the broken part of his leg.

He began by pulling the coat beneath Father’s shoulders. That turned him a little, but when Philip tried to drag him, the leg scraped against a stone. Father made a low sound in his sleep. Philip let go so quickly he nearly fell backward.

“Sorry,” he said.

He waited. Father’s eyes remained closed. The breathing continued, uneven but present.

Philip had seen men lift barrels, crates, and once a sleeping customer who had fallen asleep on a flour sack and refused to wake for closing. Father weighed more than any of them. Philip’s shoulder still ached from the crash, and his hand had become a tight, throbbing knot. He was not strong enough to carry Father in his arms. He could only make the ground do some of the work.

The land sloped gently toward the river. Above them, the Shelf Road ended in a ragged edge where the slide had taken it. Philip could see the lower route beyond a stand of pines, but the river had spilled over its banks and flooded the flat ground between. The water carried branches and pieces of bark. He could not tell how deep it was, and the current would not care if he guessed wrong.

The north road was gone. The crossing was unsafe. Malcore lay downstream.

Philip did not like the last part. It was not a place he knew how to reach on foot, and the forest stood between him and the road. But Father had said the wagon should make the trip easier than walking. The wagon was gone. The direction remained.

He needed a way to carry Father.

The cargo canvas had torn free of the wagon frame. Most of it had burned, but a long strip remained trapped beneath a wheel. Philip used a fallen branch to pull it loose. The canvas was scorched and filthy, with one edge frayed through. He shook out the ash and found two lengths of straight wood from the broken bed.

He laid them parallel on the ground. Father was longer than the space between them, and the branches were too far apart. Philip measured with his arms, moved them, and measured again. The frame wobbled when he pushed the ends. He added a crosspiece, then another, tying the wood together with torn strips from the canvas and a length of harness webbing he found under a crate.

The first knot came loose when he tested it.

Philip swore under his breath. The knot had looked tight. He pulled the strip free and watched how the cloth had folded over itself. It had slipped because he had tied it against the branch’s curve instead of around the narrow part. He tried again on a straighter section, leaving enough tail to pull hard without tearing the canvas.

The second knot held. Philip pushed down on the joint, then lifted. He leaned his weight into it with his left shoulder. The wood creaked but stayed together.

He did not trust it yet.

The next crosspiece was too short. He trimmed another branch with the sharp edge of his belt knife, keeping his bad hand away from the blade. The cut was uneven. He tested it against the opposite pole and found a gap on one side. He wedged a strip of wood into it, then tied the joint again.

His fingers cramped. He rested them on his knee until he could close them.

The second held. Philip pulled hard enough to make his sore hand throb. The branch bent but did not break.

He found one more branch, shorter and straighter than the others, and placed it across the middle. The first frame had flexed like a basket with a missing side. This one stayed square when he pushed at each corner. He sat on the canvas bed with most of his weight, then stood and checked the knots again. One had begun to slip. He tied it a third time, using his left hand to pull the strip tight.

The stretcher looked more like a pair of sticks that had agreed to remain together than a proper piece of equipment. It would have to do. Philip folded the remaining canvas over the frame to make a bed for Father. He put his coat beneath the head and kept the leg straight, as best he could.

Before risking Father’s weight, Philip laid his travel bag in the middle of the canvas and lifted one end. The poles flexed, but the knots stayed. He dragged the frame three paces over the grass, stopped, and checked every tie. The center joint had loosened. He reinforced it with a second strip of webbing and pulled until his injured hand refused to close. He finished the knot with his left, then tested it again.

Moving Father onto the stretcher took both hands and all the care Philip could manage. He used the coat to lift his shoulders, then slid the canvas under him in small pulls. Father groaned when his leg shifted. Philip stopped, waited for his breathing to settle, and tried again from a different angle. His own shoulder trembled with the effort. He could not carry Father in his arms; he could carry the frame if the frame held.

The first time he lifted the poles, Father’s shoulder slid toward the edge. Philip lowered him at once and retied the canvas around the frame. He checked each strap by pulling it before he tried again. The stretcher moved three paces across the grass. The poles bowed. The center tie held.

The river rope had been a straight line to safety. This was a line that had to carry a person. Philip tested every knot as if it were the iron ring at the bank. Then he set the stretcher down, studied how Father’s head rested against the folded coat, and shifted the cloth so the injured leg stayed straight.

Father stirred.

Philip was beside him at once. “Father?”

His eyelids moved. He tried to lift his head, then stopped with a pained breath.

“Don’t move,” Philip said. “Your leg is hurt.”

Father blinked at the sky. “Where?”

“Below the road. The wagon went over.”

“The horses?”

“They got away.”

Father closed his eyes. “Good.”

Philip leaned close enough to hear him. “Where do we go?”

“Malcore.”

“The north road is blocked. The river’s too high to cross.”

“South,” Father said. “Downstream. There’s a healer in Malcore.”

“I know.”

Father’s eyes opened again, but they did not settle on Philip. “Keep to the road.”

“Which road?”

Father’s mouth moved, and no sound came. His eyes closed. His breathing continued.

Philip waited for him to wake again. He counted ten breaths, then ten more. Father did not speak.

“A healer,” Philip repeated, so he would remember the word if his head started to swim again. “Malcore. South.”

He looked at the old road fitting still half buried near the wreck. The dagger had given him a feeling toward the trees, but it had not shown him a direction he could trust. Father had named the city and the healer. The river and the slope gave the rest. Philip did not know the way through the forest, but he knew which way downstream was.

The decision was his now. He could wait where they were and hope someone found them. He could climb back to the road and search for the horses. He could try to carry Father north, though the slide had buried the route and the river had made a wall of itself. Or he could go south, toward a place Father knew and a healer he had never met.

Philip pictured the path home: the shop door, the ledger, the warm kitchen above it. He could imagine setting Father in a room and waiting for help to arrive from Hazelwood. The picture was so clear that for a moment it felt like a choice. Then he looked again at the flooded river. The old road was buried. There was no bridge in sight. Wanting to go north did not make north possible.

Philip looked at the stretcher. Its knots held. He looked at the river. The current carried a broken branch past the flooded bank and out of sight.

“We’re going south,” he said.

It was not because Father had ordered him to. It was because the road north was blocked, the crossing was unsafe, and Father needed help. The choice frightened him. That did not make it a bad choice.

Philip gathered what little remained: the cup, the bruised books, the coin purse, the scorched dwarven box, the dagger in its sheath, the key, and the strip of canvas left from the stretcher. He wrapped the box in the least damaged shirt and tucked it into the travel bag. He tied the bag beneath Father’s head, where it could not bounce out. Then he lifted the front poles.

The stretcher moved a few inches. Father’s weight pulled hard at Philip’s shoulders. He stopped, reset his grip, and tried again.

At the edge of the trees, he looked once more at the road he could no longer take. A fox watched him from between two trunks. Its ears were upright, its tail still.

Philip bent his knees and took another step.

The fox did not follow. It only watched.
