# Chapter Twelve

## The Swamp’s False Road

At first light, the higher trail began as a line of animal prints in the mud.

Filroy led them along the edge of the swamp, where the ground rose in narrow shelves between pools of standing water. He did not hurry. Philip was grateful for that. Every step with the stretcher asked for more strength than the one before it, and he had very little to spend.

The old road ran below them. At one point, a marker stone stood at the edge of a shallow channel. Its top was tilted, and the old track seemed to continue across the mud beyond it. Philip could see a row of stone shapes beneath the water, each one partly covered by brown silt.

“That’s the road,” he said.

Filroy sniffed the air. “It was a road.”

The phrase had become familiar. Philip did not like how often the past tense appeared in the forest.

He took the branch and pressed it into the ground ahead of him. The tip sank through the mud until it struck stone. He moved the branch to the side. It sank deeper. He tried a third place, and it went through to the handle.

“The path is there,” he said.

“The mud is there too.”

“If we follow the stones—”

“You will find out how deep the mud is.”

Philip looked at the stretcher. Father had not stirred since the dry rise. The canvas was stained where his leg rested. His lips had begun to crack. They needed water, but the swamp water was full of silt and leaves, and Philip had no way to make it safe.

He wanted to use the wayknife. Father had told him the marker held traces of travel, not a promise that the route would bear them. He had just watched the old road disappear under mud. The knife could not make the path firm.

“Where do the animals go?” he asked.

Filroy walked to a bank of grass and pressed one paw against it. The ground held. He followed the line of prints up a slight rise, then turned where the reeds thinned.

“They stay above the water.”

“That’s a trail?”

“It is if you are small.”

“I’m carrying someone.”

“Then you should be glad you are not carrying two people.”

Philip did not have an answer to that. He tested the first section with his branch. The ground was firm for three steps, then soft where a narrow stream crossed under the grass. He shifted the stretcher poles higher and asked Filroy to wait while he probed a second route.

The path widened beneath a stand of alder. Philip pressed the branch down at regular intervals, counting the depth by finger marks. One spot held. The next sank past the first mark. He moved two feet to the left and tried again. This time the branch struck root.

“There,” Filroy said.

“You could have told me.”

“You were testing.”

Philip glanced at him. “I was trying to find the path.”

“Now you have.”

They moved Father across the first shelf. Philip took the front poles while Filroy walked beside the rear, keeping the canvas from catching on the grass. The fox could not lift the stretcher, but he could warn Philip when the ground dipped or when a root crossed the track.

At the narrowest point, a branch snagged Father’s coat. Philip stopped before the stretcher shifted. He lowered his end and worked the cloth free, one hand at a time. His wrapped right hand throbbed. His head pulsed in the old rhythm of the crash and the new rhythm of the day.

They rested after ten paces.

“That was ten?” Philip asked.

“If you say so.”

“I counted.”

“Then it was ten.”

Philip drank the last drops of dew from the inside of a broad leaf. It was not enough to ease his thirst. He saved the empty cup, though he had nothing to put in it. Father’s lips were dry. Philip dampened the cloth at his own collar and touched it to them. Father swallowed once without waking.

“We need water,” Philip said.

“We need many things,” Filroy replied. “Water is one of the more useful.”

“Can you find some?”

“I can find water. I can’t make it clean.”

“Then show me where it is.”

Filroy led them to a narrow seep coming from between two rocks, above the swamp line. The water was clear where it emerged, but Philip could not tell how far it had traveled through the mud. He let it run into the cup for a while, watching for grit. There was none. He smelled it, then tasted a drop.

It tasted of stone.

He filled the cup and gave Father a little, wetting his lips before letting him swallow. Philip took only one mouthful himself. The rest he saved.

The trail climbed gradually. Philip tested each section with the branch before moving the stretcher. When he was unsure, he asked Filroy. When Filroy was unsure, he tested another place. Neither of them pretended the other could guarantee what lay ahead.

By the time they reached firm ground, Father’s breathing had grown rough again. His face was hot beneath Philip’s fingers. The leg beneath the canvas had begun to swell. Philip could not tell whether the bone was broken, only that the pain was worse when they moved.

“We have to stop,” he said.

Filroy looked toward the rising land. “There’s a dry shelf above us.”

“Can we get there?”

“You can.”

Philip looked at the stretcher, then at the slope. “We’ll try.”

The last rise took them another half hour. Philip stopped twice. Once, his hand slipped and the stretcher shifted toward the swamp. Filroy braced the rear pole with his shoulder until Philip recovered his grip. He did not say he was helping. Philip did not make him say it.

At the top, the ground widened into a rough shelf of grass and stone. The forest opened below them. Far to the east, a broad corridor cut through the trees. Trunks lay broken across it. The earth had been pressed flat in a long, uneven line, with splintered branches pointing away from the path.

Philip lowered the stretcher onto the firm ground.

“Is that where it went?” he asked.

Filroy’s ears angled toward the corridor. “Something went through there.”

Philip could see no giant. He could see the damage it had left behind.

The old road had not simply been lost. Something large had found it.

Philip rested with his back against a stone. From the shelf he could see the swamp’s surface moving in small circles where rain had begun to return. The water looked calm from above. He had learned that calm was sometimes only what danger looked like at a distance.

He checked the path they had taken. Their bootprints crossed the grass, then disappeared where the ground turned to stone. Filroy’s smaller tracks ran between them and the edge. The stretcher had left a wider mark, but the canvas had not torn. Philip retied the loosened corner and checked the splint. Father’s foot remained warm, though the swelling had pushed against the cloth.

“How long can he stay like this?” Philip asked.

Filroy watched the corridor. “I know how long I can cross this shelf. I do not know how long a man can remain injured.”

Philip nodded. He would not ask the fox to know what he could not.

The corridor was broad enough for the giant to walk without turning sideways. It continued east, hidden by a low ridge. The trees at its edges leaned outward. One had been split down the trunk, and the exposed wood was pale against the wet bark.

Philip wondered whether the giant had chosen the road or only the easiest ground. The wayknife might show where it had gone. It could not tell him whether the creature knew the road from before, or whether it had found it by chance.

He put the dagger away. There was no marker close enough to ask, and no answer in the forest that he could make by wishing.
