# Chapter Seventeen

## Father’s Road

Father woke with the dawn and spoke before Philip could ask if he was ready.

“Aro saw giant tracks at a marker north of here,” he said. “Not just a trail through the woods. The waystone was carrying the pattern.”

Philip sat on the stone shelf with the wayknife across his knees. He had wiped mud from its handle, but grit still clung to the grooves. “You knew before we left Hazelwood.”

“I knew there was a warning. I did not know the whole route.”

“You knew enough to change our trip.”

Father breathed in carefully. The movement tugged at the bandage around his ribs. “Yes.”

Philip waited. He had learned that if he filled the silence, Father could hide inside it.

“Aro and I agreed to meet in Malcore,” Father continued. “He would report what he saw. I would bring the wayknife and show the Road Office the older markers. There is an old road east of the city that may lead toward a place called Stillstone Hollow.”

“May?”

“The old maps disagree. Some say the road ended at the farms. Others show it continuing east.”

“And the giant?”

“Aro thought it might be following the old road. We needed to find out whether the markers supported that.”

“You were going to tell me any of this?”

Father looked down at his hands. “I meant to give you the knife when we reached Malcore. I planned to tell you about the Slayers. About the roads. I thought we would have time to explain it properly.”

“You could have told me before we left.”

“I could have.”

“You told me we were going because we needed a change of scene.”

“That was true too.”

“It wasn’t the reason.”

“No.”

Philip picked up the wayknife and turned it over. It was a dagger. It fit his hand like a dagger, and the edge would cut skin if he pressed it against his thumb. Somewhere inside the metal was a map that did not know where it was going.

“I thought you were trying to make me into a Slayer,” Philip said.

“I wasn’t.”

“Then what were you trying to do?”

Father’s mouth opened. He closed it and began again. “I wanted you to know what I knew. I did not want the old life to decide yours.”

“You kept it from me so it wouldn’t decide for me.”

“Yes.”

“That doesn’t make sense.”

“I know.”

The words had no defense in them. Philip found that more difficult than an argument. He had prepared to be told that Father had done what he thought best. Father had said that already, in other ways. This time he only admitted the damage.

Filroy came to the shelter carrying a small, wet bundle of leaves in his mouth. He dropped it beside the cup. “The storm moved a branch across the path. The path will remain there until someone moves it. I cannot say the same for the stream.”

“You found food?” Philip asked.

“I found leaves.”

“That’s not food.”

“For me it is a message that there are still plants.”

Philip set the leaves aside. He had no appetite for messages. His stomach had gone quiet in the way it did when hunger lasted too long.

Father watched him. “There’s more.”

Philip rested his arms on his knees. “Tell me.”

“The Slayers used those roads for more than travel. We found creatures by reading where they had gone. Sometimes we moved markers to guide them toward a hunt. Sometimes we used the road to get people out of the way.”

“You used the marks to send them somewhere.”

“We used the roads. The marks only told us what had happened. The choice was ours.”

“Was that why the Slayers ended?”

“Partly. The war ended. We had saved people. We had also made choices for towns that did not always want our help. Some hunters thought the danger justified whatever worked. Others thought that was how a protector became another kind of threat.”

“Which side were you on?”

Father took a long time to answer. “At the beginning, I thought a road was a tool. If the marker showed where a creature had gone, we could get people out of the way or put hunters where they needed to be.”

“And later?”

“Later I saw that the same reading could be used for both. The marker did not know whether we were clearing a road or setting a trap. We did.”

“Did you ever move danger without telling the people near it?”

Father’s thumb pressed into his palm. “Yes.”

“Did they ever find out?”

“I don’t know.”

Philip looked toward the bridge entrance. Filroy had returned to the stream’s edge, keeping watch. Aro’s tracks were still in the wet ground above them. The road held all kinds of records. None of them recorded what a person had chosen not to say.

Father’s gaze went to the storm-dark entrance. “I believed I could tell the difference each time.”

Philip waited.

“I was not always right.”

Father rubbed the heel of his hand against his brow. The bandage shifted, and Philip reached to fix it. Father let him. “We once moved a marker to draw a beast toward an empty quarry. It was meant to keep it away from a village. The beast turned before it reached the quarry. We got the village out in time, but we had not warned them what was coming.”

“You could have warned them.”

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t you?”

“We thought we had control of the route. We thought telling them would make them panic.”

“Did it?”

“The creature turned. The people saw it. They moved.”

Philip folded the edge of the blanket. “You decided what they needed to know.”

Father looked at him. “I did.”

The answer made the lesson of the road feel less distant. Father had not only hidden the current danger. He had learned to call withholding information protection long before Philip was old enough to question it.

“Why leave?” Philip asked.

Father shifted, then stopped when his leg moved. Philip put a hand out, but Father raised his palm to keep him from touching the splint.

“I was away when your mother died,” he said. “The raid came while I was with the Slayers. I came home after. By then there was nothing I could change.”

Philip had known the fact of her death for as long as he could remember, though no one had ever sat him down and given it a shape. He knew she had died in the orc raid. He knew Father had been away. He did not know where the Slayers had been or how long Father had taken to get home, and he did not ask for every detail at once.

“Did you hear about the raid before you came back?”

“I heard after it had happened.” Father looked down at the blanket. “I was too far away to return in time.”

“Did you think you could have stopped it?”

“For a long time.”

“Could you?”

Father looked at him. “I don’t know. I was not there. That is the part I cannot answer.”

Philip wanted a cleaner answer. He wanted an answer that placed blame somewhere he could point to, or lifted it away. Father had neither. The silence around his mother had never been empty; it had been full of things neither of them knew how to say.

Philip knew she had died. He knew there had been an orc raid. He had not known Father was away because of the Slayers. In the stories of his childhood she had been a name at the edge of photographs, a woman whose shawl Father kept folded in the top drawer. Philip had built no picture of the day she died. Now the empty place had a road leading away from it.

“You think it was your fault.”

“I think I was not there.”

“That isn’t the same thing.”

“No.” Father pressed his thumb into his palm. “It still mattered.”

Philip had wanted to ask why Father had chosen the Slayers over his family. The question arrived sharp and ready. Then he saw the way Father held himself still, as if he were bracing against a second injury. Philip could ask later. Or not. The answer would not bring his mother back or make Father’s absence smaller.

“You didn’t tell me because you thought the old work would take me away too?”

“I thought if I made you strong enough, nothing would.”

“Strong enough to fight.”

“That was what I understood.”

“And if I had just stayed in the shop?”

“I hoped you would.” Father looked at the knife between them. “I wanted you to have the choice I did not give myself. But I kept the truth from you while I waited for you to make it.”

Philip looked at the knife’s grooves. “You keep wanting me to be prepared, but you don’t tell me what for.”

“I know.”

“I can’t help you if you hide the part that matters.”

“I know.”

“You have to let me decide what I can carry.”

Father met his eyes. “I will try.”

“And if the Road Office says the old route is too dangerous?”

“We listen to what they know.”

“And if they’re wrong?”

“We tell them what we saw.”

“Even if they don’t believe us?”

Father looked toward the knife. “Then we find someone who will check.”

It was the first time Father had described a plan that did not depend on Philip obeying him. It was also only a plan for the next few hours. Philip would take that much.

It was not an apology. It was not enough. It was more than he had said before.

They agreed to move after Philip had rested and the stream began to fall. Father would tell him what he remembered about the route, and Philip would tell Father what he saw, even if he was unsure. Filroy would choose the dry ground when the trail split. No one promised the route would be safe.

While Philip folded the blanket, Filroy found a set of boot prints in the soft earth above the bridge. They were spaced evenly, and one heel had left a half-moon nick in the mud.

“Aro?” Philip asked.

“I know one person who walks as if every footprint is an introduction,” Father said.

The prints came from the south road. They were fresh enough that rain had not filled them.

Father looked at Philip. “He is catching up.”

Philip tucked the wayknife into his belt. “Then we should hear what he knows.”
