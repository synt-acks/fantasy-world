# Chapter Seven

## The Berry Tree

Philip did not sleep so much as stop noticing the dark for a while.

He sat close to Father with the coat drawn over both their shoulders. Every time Father’s breathing changed, Philip opened his eyes. Every time the fire settled with a crack, he opened them again. Eventually the night thinned enough that the trees became shapes instead of a wall.

His stomach hurt. He had not eaten since the stew at home. The thought of food kept returning with the stubbornness of a customer who had already been told the shop was closed.

Father did not wake.

By the time the first gray light reached the clearing, hunger had become more urgent than fear. Philip could not keep waiting for the world to improve on its own. He stood, slowly, and looked toward the tree line.

He checked Father once more before leaving. The cloth at his hairline had dried stiff, and Philip loosened it enough to see whether the cut had begun bleeding again. It had not. He listened for breath, counted five, then counted five more. Father’s chest rose beneath the coat. Philip pressed two fingers to the inside of his wrist and felt a pulse, faint but steady.

Philip had no clean water and no food he could find. The horses were gone. The wagon had burned. If he went searching for help, Father would be alone with a head wound and a leg that lay at the wrong angle. If he stayed, they would both remain hungry.

He considered climbing down toward the river. The bank was hidden by brush and the slope dropped sharply below the clearing. A fall would leave Father alone with one injured boy instead of one injured boy and a hungry one. Philip decided against it. Water was necessary. So was getting there alive.

“I’ll be close,” Philip told him.

He stepped away only as far as he could still see Father between the wreckage and the scrub. The nearest trees began at the edge of the clearing. He looked for anything he recognized: familiar leaves, low branches, signs of an animal path. He knew enough about plants to tell a berry from a nut and not enough to tell every berry from every other berry. It was a narrow sort of knowledge, but it was the sort he had.

A squirrel darted across a fallen branch.

Philip followed it.

The squirrel climbed a tree, ran along a limb, and stopped with its tail upright. Philip tried to circle around the trunk without making a sound. He had no snare and no idea how to make one. His plan was to get close enough to catch it by hand, which was not much of a plan, but hunger had been known to improve bad ideas.

The squirrel saw him. It left by a route Philip had not noticed.

He searched beneath the roots and checked the next tree. The squirrel reappeared above him, holding something in its mouth. Philip took a step. It vanished again.

Philip tried to guess where it would come down. He chose a patch of ground below the next tree and waited, keeping still. The squirrel appeared on a branch behind him. It dropped to a root, crossed the clearing, and disappeared into a hole in a stump. Philip watched the stump for a long moment. He did not reach inside. A person could make a poor situation worse by grabbing at whatever lived in it.

He followed it twice more before he understood that he was doing exactly what it wanted. The squirrel was either leading him away from the clearing or had discovered the most reliable form of entertainment in the forest.

Philip went back.

He found a patch of dark berries on a low bush near the broken road. The fruit grew in small clusters, with tiny hairs along the stems. He had seen them in baskets at the shop and knew their taste, though he could not remember what the supplier called them. He ate one. It was tart and soft, and it did not make his mouth numb.

That was not proof of safety. It was the best test he had.

He waited. Nothing happened except that his mouth watered for another. After a few minutes he ate one more, then one more. He kept the rest separate until he was sure they were the same kind. The shop had taught him that a label was only useful if it matched the thing inside the basket.

He gathered a handful and left the rest on the bush. The berries were not a meal, but they gave his stomach something to consider besides itself.

Philip filled the bent cup with berries, then carried it to the edge of the clearing and looked for water. The river was visible only in a few flashes through the trees. Between them and it lay a slope of loose stone and thorn. He could not see where the bank began, and the sound of the current came from farther away than he expected.

He tried collecting dew from the broad leaves. A few drops gathered in the bottom of the cup, each one carrying a fleck of dirt. He wiped the cup clean and tried again, holding it beneath a low branch while the last water shook loose. The handful he collected would not have filled a spoon. He drank it anyway, then regretted how little it changed.

On the return, he checked the ground for tracks. The soil near the trees had been pressed by small paws and by something much heavier. The larger marks were too broken to read. Philip crouched to measure one against his hand, then stopped when his head began to throb. He could not tell whether it was a footprint or a place where the rain had washed away the earth.

He left it alone. He had no reason to put his injured hand in a hole he could not understand.

A bramble had climbed the tree, and higher clusters hung just beyond his reach. Philip could reach them if he climbed the trunk. He looked at the tree, then at his swollen right hand.

He could try with the other hand. He could use the lower limbs. He could stop if it hurt too much.

That was three plans, which was more than he had had for the squirrel.

The first branch held. Philip put his foot against the trunk and pulled himself up. The second branch bent beneath him. He eased his weight back and shifted to a thicker limb. He moved slowly, testing each hold before he trusted it. The tree was old, its bark rough enough to catch his sleeve. It was also taller than it had looked from the ground, which was a common quality among trees.

He reached the fruit. His fingers closed around a cluster. The branch flexed.

Philip stopped with the berries in his hand and studied the trunk below him. The way down looked different from the way up. This was a familiar trick of heights: they made every path seem simpler from the bottom, where the ground was kind enough to wait.

He could reach another cluster if he moved out along the branch. He could also come down with what he had. The berries in his fist were enough for one small meal, not enough for the journey. Still, he was already shaking, and the tree had begun to creak.

Philip stopped moving. The limb creaked under him. He pressed his body toward the trunk and waited for the sound to fade.

The wind moved through the leaves. Somewhere above, a bird gave a sharp call and flew away.

Then the ground trembled.

It was faint, more a shiver through the tree than a shake. Philip’s cheek brushed the bark. A few leaves drifted down. At the edge of the clearing, birds burst from the brush all at once, their wings beating through the branches.

Philip held on and listened.

Nothing followed. No roar. No crash. Only the settling leaves and the distant river.

He climbed down a little too quickly. A twig snapped under his boot. He slipped, struck his right hand against the trunk, and dropped the last few feet onto the ground.

Pain shot through his knuckles. He sat where he landed and held his hand against his chest until the sharpness passed. The berries were still in his other hand, crushed but mostly whole.

The tree had not killed him. It had merely made its opinion clear.

Philip gathered the fruit that had fallen and walked back to Father. He set a few berries beside him, then ate the rest one at a time. He could have climbed again. There were more berries higher up. But his hand was worse, his head was throbbing, and the clearing seemed to tilt if he turned too fast.

He stopped.

The decision disappointed him. It also felt sensible. There was a difference between learning he could climb a tree and pretending the first climb had made him an expert.

He checked his hand. The swelling had worsened, and the skin across one knuckle had split again. He brushed grit from it as best he could, then tied the cleanest corner of the old cloth around it. The tin cup was empty. He saved it anyway, in case they found water later.

The ground shivered once more, too slight to move the stones but enough to set a tin cup rolling through the grass. Philip caught it with his foot. He looked toward the trees. Nothing moved except the branches.

When dawn finally reached the clearing, it showed him the north road.

A slide of rock and dirt had buried the narrow shelf behind them. The river below had swollen into a muddy, fast-moving band. Even if the climb back up were possible, there was no clear way to turn north and cross the water.

He climbed as far as the base of the slide and looked for a gap. The rocks were slick with fresh soil. One had rolled into the road with enough force to break the stone beside it. Philip tested the edge with his boot and felt gravel give way. He backed down. From there he could see the river’s surface, which carried branches and foam in the same direction: south.

Philip knelt beside Father. “I’m going to find a way out.”

Father did not stir.

Philip looked once at the fallen road, then at the forest to the south. The berries had not made him strong. They had only made it possible to stand up and keep looking.
