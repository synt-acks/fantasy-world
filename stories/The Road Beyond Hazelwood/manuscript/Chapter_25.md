# Chapter Twenty-Five

## What Strength Costs

Father was awake when Philip returned to the infirmary.

The healer had left a bowl of broth on the stool beside his bed. Father had eaten half of it. He was trying to sit up, though the splint and the bandage around his ribs made the effort slow.

“The roadwarden has given us a crew,” Philip said. “They’re clearing the barrier and the drainage cut.”

“And the giant?”

“Still moving toward the outer farms.”

Father looked at the dagger on Philip’s belt. “Give me the knife.”

Philip stayed where he was. “Why?”

“You’re not going back out.”

“The roadwarden asked me to help guide the crew.”

“You have a head wound and a hand that can barely close.”

“I can point at a map.”

“You can fall over. You nearly did twice on the road.”

“I did fall over once.”

“That is not an argument in your favor.”

Father reached for the knife. Philip stepped back. The movement pulled at Father’s leg and pain caught his face. He stopped, breathing through his teeth.

The healer was at the other end of the ward. Other patients turned toward the sound, then tried not to look. People in infirmaries had many ways of pretending not to hear a family argument. The most common was to make a great show of examining the ceiling.

“You said you would share what you knew,” Philip said.

“I am sharing this: you are hurt, and the giant is dangerous.”

“You knew the giant was moving before we left Hazelwood.”

“I did not know it was this close.”

“You knew there was a danger. You didn’t tell me. You told me to fight bullies, but when I have something useful to say about a road, you want me sent away.”

Father’s hand lowered to the blanket. “This is different.”

“How?”

“You could be killed.”

“So could the people outside the wall.”

“That is why the guards are there.”

“And the roadworker? And Aro? And the people clearing the turn?”

Father did not answer.

Philip’s voice shook. He hated that it did. “You keep telling me to be brave in the way you understand. When I do something else, you call it a risk.”

“You are fifteen.”

“I was fifteen when you decided to bring me past the Shelf Road without telling me what you knew.”

The words seemed to stop Father more completely than the pain had. His face changed. Not much, but enough for Philip to see that the argument had reached the place both of them had been avoiding.

“I thought I could keep you safe,” Father said.

“By choosing for me.”

“By keeping you out of the old life.”

“You don’t get to keep me out of something by hiding it from me.”

Father looked toward the window. Beyond it, the city’s east wall cut a pale line across the sky. “Your mother died while I was away.”

“You told me.”

“I thought if I brought the Slayer work into our house, it would take you away too. I thought the safest thing was to leave the past where it was.”

“But it wasn’t where it was. It was on the road with us.”

Father closed his eyes. “I know.”

“You can’t make me safe by making every choice before I know there’s a choice.”

“I was afraid.”

The admission came quietly. It was not an excuse. Father looked at the blanket as if the words had cost him more than the journey had.

“I was afraid I would lose you as I lost her,” he said. “I thought if you learned to fight, you would be harder to hurt. I thought if you never learned about the Slayers, you would never want their life. I told myself those things could fit together.”

“They don’t.”

“No.”

Philip touched the dagger’s hilt. “This was a gift. You gave it to me because you wanted me to have a choice. You can’t give it back to yourself when I make one.”

Father looked up. His hand moved toward the knife again, then stopped on the blanket. “You’re right.”

“I’m going with the crew. I’ll stay behind the workers. I’ll tell them what I saw, and I’ll stop if I can’t stand.”

Father’s jaw tightened. He looked as if he wanted to forbid it. Then his shoulders sank a little.

“I don’t like it.”

“I know.”

“I’m still afraid.”

“I know that too.”

Father drew a slow breath. “Then go. Tell me what you learn when you return.”

Philip had expected to feel relief. Instead, the permission sat between them like a narrow plank laid across a gap. Father had not told him to go away; he had also not said he trusted the plan. Philip could not demand that the fear vanish before he moved.

“I’ll come back,” Philip said.

“I know.”

Father’s gaze stayed on the knife. “I should have told you about the road before we left. I should have told you what Aro saw.”

“Yes.”

“I’m not asking you to stop being angry.”

“Good.”

The healer arrived with a cup of water and a strip of clean cloth. She inspected Philip’s scalp and asked him to follow her finger with his eyes. He did, though the finger seemed to leave a faint trail after it stopped.

“You are not going near the front line,” she said. “You are not reading that knife again unless somebody with a steadier head is there to catch you.”

“I can stay behind the workers.”

“You can stay behind the workers and drink the water.”

Philip took the cup. The water tasted clean. He drank slowly, then gave the cup back when she told him to stop. Father watched without trying to take it from him.

Philip nodded. He had not forgiven Father. Father had not become unafraid. But the knife remained at Philip’s belt, and Father had let him keep it.

The warning bell sounded from the east wall.

Once. Then twice.

Philip finished the water. The healer checked the bandage once more, then let him go.
