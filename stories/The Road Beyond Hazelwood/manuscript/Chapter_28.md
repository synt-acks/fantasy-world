# Chapter Twenty-Eight

## The Hollow Beyond

Stillstone Hollow opened beneath the eastern ridge.

The basalt walls formed a shallow bowl ringed by old pines. Water ran down the rock in narrow threads, but the ground at the center was dry. A shelf of stone overhung one side, deep enough to shelter the giant from the rain.

The giant entered without ceremony. It stood in the center of the hollow and lowered itself onto the stone. The earth trembled once as its weight settled. Then it became still.

The people who had followed at a distance stopped among the trees. No one approached. No one called to it. The giant’s head rested against the rock, its arms folded near its chest. It did not seem peaceful. It seemed exhausted.

One scout began to move down the ridge. The roadwarden caught his sleeve and pointed to the giant’s hand. Its fingers had shifted against the stone. The scout stopped. After a moment the giant drew one slow breath, lifted its head, and pressed its shoulder against the overhang. Small pieces of grit fell from the basalt.

Everyone moved back another dozen paces.

The giant settled again. The hollow held. That was the only conclusion the watchers were willing to make.

The roadwarden sent two scouts to watch from the ridge and ordered the clearing crew to mark the route for future repair. The city would keep the outer road open and post a warning at the old turn. The giant had reached shelter. The road would still need tending after it was gone, or after it moved on.

She posted guards on the road, not at the hollow’s entrance. Their orders were to keep travelers away and send word if the giant moved. Aro agreed to stay until the first watch changed. He was not asked to shoot, and he did not ask for permission to approach.

Aro stood beside Philip, his bow slung over his shoulder.

“You were right to make us wait,” he said.

“We didn’t know enough.”

“We still don’t know everything.”

“No.”

Aro looked toward the hollow. “My friend would have wanted me to tell his story honestly.”

“You did.”

“Not the whole of it.” Aro’s voice lowered. “I told people what he did. I left out how long he was afraid before he went in. I left out that I told him not to. I made the ending sound like proof that courage was enough.”

Philip thought of the man inside the Alcrog, and of the hunter who had watched him die after the beast fell. “It was courage.”

“Yes. It was also a choice with a cost.”

Philip nodded. That was the part a story could keep and still make room for the person who had lived it.

Filroy stood at the edge of the trees. He watched the hollow, then the road leading back toward Malcore. His den lay somewhere beyond the forest, damaged or not. He had not said whether he would go back to it. Foxes were not known for announcing their schedules.

“You’re leaving,” Philip said.

“I am going home.”

“Will you come to Malcore?”

“No.”

“Will I see you again?”

Filroy looked at him. “You will be in a forest again someday.”

“That doesn’t answer me.”

“It is the answer I have.”

Philip held out the last piece of bread from Aro’s pack. Filroy sniffed it and turned away.

“You can keep it,” Philip said.

“I can find food.”

“I know.”

Filroy trotted between the trees, paused once to look back, then disappeared into the brush.

Father was taken to the infirmary again. The healer said his leg would need weeks to mend and his head required continued watching. He would not ride home for some time. The city had spare rooms for travelers whose families were being treated, and Philip slept in one of them with clean sheets and a bowl of broth.

The first night, he woke whenever a bell rang. The city had many bells, and each one seemed to mean the giant had moved again. A nurse came in after the third time and showed him the watch board outside the ward. The marks beside the eastern post had not changed. The giant was still in the hollow.

Philip slept better after that, though not well. At dawn he woke with his hand clenched around the edge of the blanket. The fingers were stiff, but they opened. The healer said the bruising would take time and the cut at his scalp was clean. She gave him bread and broth before she let him visit Father.

Father had spent most of the night asleep. The healer had set his lower leg and secured it with a firm splint. She repeated that he would need weeks, not days, and that he was not to walk on it. Father looked at the splint as if it had insulted him personally.

“It has a point,” Philip said.

“What point?”

“It’s keeping you in bed.”

“Then it’s a poor argument.”

The roadwarden came to see them the next morning. She brought the survey sheet and asked Father to mark the old causeway from memory. Father corrected two turns and admitted he was uncertain about a third. Philip added the visible track and the marker readings in a different color. The two lines did not perfectly overlap. The roadwarden kept both.

“We’ll send a crew to maintain the old turn,” she said. “If the giant leaves the hollow, we’ll need to know whether it uses the road again.”

“And if it doesn’t?” Philip asked.

“Then we will have repaired a road people can use.”

The roadwarden rolled the survey sheet and tucked it under her arm. “This road was allowed to disappear because it stopped being useful to the people who kept the records. We can at least make sure the next person knows where it goes.”

Philip watched her return to the gatehouse. Outside the wall, carts waited to go home or to find a different road. The city was not finished being frightened, but it had begun to make a list of what needed repairing.

Over the next days, Father woke more often. They spoke in pieces. Some conversations ended in silence; others ended because Father needed sleep. He told Philip about the Slayers’ routes, about roads used to evacuate villages, and about times the same roads had been used to lead beasts toward a trap. Philip asked questions. Father answered what he could and admitted when he did not remember.

He apologized on the third morning.

“I told you fighting was the only way to be strong,” Father said. “Then I kept the truth from you because I thought I could protect you by choosing what you knew. I was wrong.”

Philip looked at the bandage on Father’s head. “I’m still angry.”

“You should be.”

“I don’t want to join the Slayers.”

“There are no Slayers to join.”

“You know what I mean.”

Father nodded. “Then you won’t.”

“I want to learn how to read the roads. And I want to learn enough to get away if someone corners me again.”

“I can teach you both.”

“When your leg works.”

“That may take a while.”

“The shop can wait.”

Father’s eyes moved to him. “You still want the shop?”

“Yes.”

“Good.” Father gave a small, tired smile. “It is a fine place to learn how much people will pay for a sack of flour.”

“They usually pay the marked price.”

“That is what they tell you.”

Philip laughed. It surprised him. The laugh hurt his ribs, though not enough to stop it.

Weeks later, when Father could sit in a cart without the healer objecting, Philip stood beyond Malcore’s gate and looked north. The road wound away between farms and low ridges. It would lead to the river, to the forest, and eventually to Hazelwood.

By then, Aro had gone back to the Road Office with a report of his own. He had left the Alcrog hide behind for a day while it dried, then put it on again. Philip had asked whether he would keep telling the story of his friend.

“Yes,” Aro had said. “But I’ll tell the part where he was afraid. And the part where I was.”

Philip had not told him that this was better. Aro knew.

The wayknife rested in its box at his side. It did not point home. It did not choose for him.

Philip knew the road would take time, and that time would include sore hands, bad weather, and probably at least one argument about how much flour to buy. He also knew he would travel it when Father was ready.

The first journey beyond Hazelwood had not made him a warrior. It had taught him that being afraid did not take a choice away, and that the best road was often the one people made possible together.

He turned back toward the gate. Father waited inside, leaning on a cane, impatient with the healer and trying not to show it.

“Are you coming?” Father called.

“I’m looking.”

“At what?”

Philip glanced once more at the road.

“Where it goes.”
