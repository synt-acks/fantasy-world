# Chapter Four

## Shelf Road

The first hour out of Hazelwood passed beneath a sky the color of old tin.

On ordinary trips, Father let Philip choose which side of the bench he wanted and asked him to read the delivery list aloud. Philip would call the stops while Father watched the road, and they would argue about whether a cracked jar counted as a loss if the buyer had not yet seen it. The arguments had rules. They could be settled by the ledger, or by asking the buyer, or by Father saying that a jar was a jar no matter how philosophical it became.

This morning Father had not brought the delivery list. He had brought two maps and kept one beneath his coat. Philip had seen the corner of the older one when Father folded it. It was drawn on yellowed paper, with a route marked in a darker ink than the surrounding roads.

“You brought two maps,” Philip said.

Father glanced down. “One is a backup.”

“Which one is the backup?”

“The newer one.”

“Then the older one is the one you trust.”

“The older one is the one I know.”

Philip looked out at the gray road. “Those are different things.”

“Sometimes.”

Father tucked the corner of the older map farther beneath his coat. Philip did not ask to see it. He had learned that a refusal could begin before a question was finished.

Philip sat beside Father on the wagon bench with his bag between his boots. The horses drew them south at an unhurried trot. The river flashed through gaps in the trees to their right, broad and brown beneath the morning mist. Behind them, the road narrowed until Hazelwood was no more than a low smudge on the northern horizon.

The wagon was the one Father used for the shop’s largest orders: two bench seats, a canvas cover rolled above the load, and an iron-banded box beneath the passenger bench for tools. The boards smelled of old grain and damp rope. Philip knew which plank squeaked, which latch needed a lift before it would close, and where Father kept the spare wheel pins. Those details made the wagon feel more like an extension of the shop than a vehicle carrying them away from it.

Father drove without speaking. Philip tried reading, but the wagon had a talent for finding every stone on the road. After the same line jumped beneath his eyes for the fourth time, he closed the book.

“You could have slept another hour,” Father said.

“You woke me before dawn.”

“You said you were ready.”

“I said I was coming.”

Father glanced at him, then back at the road. “You did.”

The answer left no room for an argument. Philip watched the wheel turn. He had never been bothered by a quiet ride before. Usually he read until the road or the book made him sleepy. Today the silence had too much space in it.

“How long until Malcore?” he asked.

“A few days, if the roads stay passable.”

“And if they don’t?”

“Then we find another way.”

“That sounds like something you say when you don’t know.”

Father’s mouth twitched. “It is.”

They stopped once to water the horses at a stream that crossed the road. Philip held the bridle of the nearer animal while Father checked the wagon wheels and tightened a strap on the load. The animals drank deeply. One lifted its head and blew water across Philip’s sleeve.

“That was deliberate,” Philip said.

“It was thirsty.”

“It could have been thirsty in a less personal direction.”

Father rubbed the horse’s neck. “You’ll have to discuss that with it.”

Philip held the reins while Father unfolded the newer map on the wagon seat. The map showed the river, the lower road, and a few bridges. The old South Road was drawn in a thin gray line that vanished and returned as if its maker had been uncertain the road still existed.

“The lower track follows the water,” Philip said. “Why does it flood if it’s lower?”

“Because the river rises.”

“Then it isn’t much of a road.”

“It works when it’s dry.”

“That is not the same thing.”

Father folded the map. “No. It isn’t.”

He said it as if Philip had proved something, though neither of them had won an argument.

They moved on. By midmorning, the track had climbed from the riverbank and begun to follow the side of a long ridge. The land fell sharply to the west. On the other side, the ground rose into dark slopes patched with pine. The wagon wheels clicked over old stone set into the road. Here and there, the stones had shifted enough to leave gaps where grass had grown through.

Clouds had gathered over the south since dawn, dark underneath and pale at the edges. Father checked them whenever the road straightened. He said nothing about the weather, but he had begun to drive faster.

“This is the Shelf Road?” Philip asked.

“The old South Road. It runs above the ravine until it meets the lower track.”

“Why take the old road?”

“It’s shorter.”

“By how much?”

“A day, perhaps.”

Philip looked over the edge. “That seems like a lot to save.”

“The lower track floods when the river rises. The longer way is passable, but it would put us farther from Malcore tonight.”

“And we need to get there quickly?”

Father adjusted the reins. “We have reasons to go.”

Philip waited. His father did not add any.

The ridge road turned around a shoulder of rock. Ahead, a low stone wall guarded the outer edge. Its upper stones were pale with moss, and a row of old drainage cuts ran beneath them. Water trickled from one cut and crossed the road in a thin silver line. The rain must have fallen sometime in the night. Philip saw where it had carried grit and small leaves from the higher slope and piled them against the road’s edge.

There were shallow hollows in the packed dirt between the stones. Rainwater had collected in them, making their outlines hard to see. They were too broad for hoofprints and too irregular for wheels. A larger weight had pressed there, once or several times, though Philip could not tell when. He remembered the shepherd’s account of a giant and looked back toward the mountain.

“Are those from it?” he asked.

Father followed his gaze. “Could be.”

“You sound certain.”

“I’m not.”

He drew the horses closer to the inner wall. Philip kept watching the marks until a bend hid them.

“The ditch is blocked,” he said.

Father slowed the horses. “Where?”

Philip pointed. “There. And the water’s running under the stones instead of through the drain.”

His father studied the road. He guided the wagon closer to the inside wall, away from the ravine. The horses shortened their steps.

“We’ll take it slowly,” he said.

The wagon crept forward. Philip watched the stream of water running through the next drainage cut and tried to judge how much had gone beneath the road instead. The ditch was full of leaves and gravel. A proper road should have carried water away. This one seemed to be storing it for later.

“Could we turn back?” Philip asked.

“Not easily.”

“We could go down to the river road.”

“That would add a day, and the lower track may be flooded.”

“We haven’t checked.”

Father’s eyes stayed on the road. “No.”

“Then we don’t know it’s flooded.”

“We also don’t know the Shelf Road is unsafe.”

Philip looked at the broken drains. “We know it’s old.”

“Old roads can be sound.”

“And unsound ones can look fine until they aren’t.”

Father glanced at him. For a second Philip thought he might turn the wagon around. Instead, Father eased the reins and kept the horses close to the inner wall. “We’ll go one stretch at a time.”

It was a practical answer. It was also the kind of answer Philip wished his father had given him about the trip.

Philip looked along the broken wall. A flat length of dark iron had been set into the stone at shoulder height, its surface pitted and marked with narrow grooves. A piece of the stone around it had cracked away. The iron did not look like a sign or a hinge. It looked like part of a much older machine whose purpose had been forgotten when it stopped moving.

“What’s that?”

Father glanced at the fitting. His hand tightened on the reins.

“Road iron.”

“What does it do?”

“Nothing now.”

“Was it part of the drain?”

“No.”

Father looked ahead again. The wagon crept forward. Philip tried to read the grooves, but their pattern meant nothing to him. He remembered the box in Father’s travel bag, secured beneath the bench. He did not ask whether the fitting had anything to do with it. He had already learned what answers he would get.

The horses passed the first drain. The next was clear. At the third, water slipped through the cut and disappeared beneath the wall. The road dipped slightly where the stone had settled. Philip felt the wagon tilt toward the ravine.

“Father.”

“I see it.”

“The outside stones—”

The nearer wheel struck a rut. The wagon lurched. One of the horses tossed its head, and the harness creaked.

“Easy,” Father said, drawing the reins in.

Something cracked beneath them.

The sound was no louder than a board splitting, but the road shifted under the wagon. Philip looked down at the wheel. A thin line opened between the road and the outer wall, water darkening the seam. The wheel dropped half a handspan.

Philip felt the wagon lean before he saw the ground shift. The outer stones were not merely loose; the whole edge had settled outward. Beneath the road, water had been running where there should have been packed earth. It had washed away the support a little at a time, and the wagon had supplied the final weight.

“Stop!” Philip shouted.

Father pulled hard on the reins. The horses checked their pace. For a heartbeat, the wagon held still, leaning above the ravine. Philip felt his stomach move as if it had missed the warning.

Then the shoulder collapsed.

Stone and earth gave way beneath the wheels. The wagon tipped toward the open slope. Father released one rein and seized Philip by the back of his coat.

“Out!”

He shoved Philip toward the rock wall. Philip struck the ground on his shoulder and rolled against the inside ledge. The impact knocked the breath out of him. He tried to sit up.

The wagon lurched past.

Father was still on the bench, both hands fighting the reins. The horses pulled sideways, panicked by the broken road. One harness strap snapped. The wagon’s outer wheel lifted free of the ground; for a moment the whole load hung at an angle, its crates sliding toward the open side.

Father looked back at Philip. Their eyes met.

The wagon dropped.

The world tipped with it. A horse broke from its traces and galloped along the road, reins whipping behind. The other followed, vanishing around the bend. The wagon struck the ravine slope, bounced once, and rolled out of sight. A burst of wood cracked below. Then came a duller impact and a wash of orange light.

Philip pushed himself onto his knees. The edge of the road was crumbling around him. He could hear a horse somewhere above, snorting and running. He could hear the river far below. He could hear his own breath, fast and shallow, but not his father.

He crawled toward the broken edge.

“Father!”

The slope beneath him shivered. A stone the size of a loaf tumbled down and vanished. Philip reached for the nearest root. His fingers slipped over the dirt.

He tried to stand. His legs did not agree. The sky had turned bright at its center and dark at the edges. He felt blood run from his hairline to his temple. When he touched it, his hand came away wet.

Below, the wagon burned among the rocks.

Philip heard one more crack, though he could not tell whether it came from the wreckage, the road, or inside his head. He tried to call Father again. The word broke apart before it reached the air.

The ridge tilted. The river rose to meet it.

Then everything went dark.
