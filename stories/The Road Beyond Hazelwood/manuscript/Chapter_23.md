# Chapter Twenty-Three

## The Road Office

Malcore’s Road Office occupied three rooms above the south gatehouse.

The first held maps, ledgers, and a table too large for the room. The second held people trying to use the table at once. The third held the roadwarden, who had placed both hands on a map of the city and was listening to a clerk read the latest bell reports.

The clerk read the reports in a quick, practiced voice. One bell from the east watch. A second from the outer farms. No injuries reported at the wall. Two carts delayed at the north gate. A request for more sandbags at the river culvert. The facts came in without an order that would make them comforting.

The roadwarden waited until the clerk finished, then asked the same question twice: “How certain is the direction?” The first messenger had said south. The second had said southeast. The third had reported the giant had stopped.

“It may have stopped,” the clerk said. “Or the watcher lost sight of it.”

“Write that,” the roadwarden replied. “Do not shorten it to ‘stopped.’”

Philip expected the roadwarden to be older. She was a square-shouldered woman with short gray hair and ink on the side of one thumb. Her coat had no ornament except a brass road pin. She looked at Aro, then at Philip, then at the mud and dried blood on both of them.

“You came from the north road?” she asked.

“From the Shelf Road,” Aro said. “The giant has been following a route through the forest. We saw it on the ridge.”

“And its direction?”

“South.”

“Toward the city?”

“Toward the river road.”

Philip stepped closer to the map. “It keeps to high ground. At the ridge it avoided the flooded hollow and turned east along the basalt rise.”

The roadwarden looked at him. “You saw that?”

“We watched it. We also found old markers along the causeway.”

Aro placed the damaged map on the table. “The current marker readings show a strong route south. The old eastward line is weak.”

“Then why should we consider it?”

“Because the giant’s passage is stronger than the older traffic,” Philip said. “The wayknife reads what traveled most. It doesn’t tell us where the giant means to go.”

The roadwarden’s eyes moved to the dagger at his belt. “How many readings did you make?”

“Two. Aro read the marker on the ridge; I read the intact causeway marker. His reading showed a strong route south. At the causeway, I felt that heavy trace over a lighter, more regular one running east.”

“And the fallen marker beyond it?”

“It was damaged. I didn’t read it.”

The roadwarden glanced at Aro. “You agree with that account?”

Aro hesitated. “I agree that the knife cannot tell us why the giant is moving. I believe the city is at risk if we wait too long.”

“Those can both be true,” the roadwarden said. “We have a wall to protect and people in the outer farms. We also have no clear access to the east service road. The old road is listed as blocked by private land and a failed drainage cut.”

“We could inspect it,” Philip said.

“A roadworker can inspect it. You need medical attention.”

“I’ll get it. My father is here with the healer. I can show the roadworker where the stone turn is.”

Father’s voice came from the doorway. “He should not be sent out again.”

Philip turned. The healer had brought Father in on a wheeled cot. His leg was enclosed in a proper splint, his head wrapped cleanly. He looked exhausted, but awake.

“I can speak for myself,” Philip said.

The room went quiet. Father closed his eyes briefly.

“You’re right,” he said. “I’m sorry.”

The roadwarden pointed to a chair. “Your father is going back to the infirmary. You may speak with a roadworker for an hour, provided the healer agrees. You will not go beyond the outer farms without an escort.”

“That’s enough,” Philip said.

The roadwarden tapped the map. “The east road crosses a drainage cut. If the cut has failed, a giant cannot pass without making it worse. If it is blocked by fences, the farms will be in its path. I will not order people to clear it based on a faint marker and a boy’s impression.”

The roadwarden pulled a second map from beneath the first. This one showed the farms by name, with fields and wells marked in red. A black line stopped at the drainage cut. “If the giant turns at the wall, it will enter these fields. If we clear the east road and it does not take it, we may expose the workers without gaining anything.”

“If you don’t clear it, there won’t be a choice,” Philip said. “We don’t know where it wants to go. We know the turn is blocked, and we know it keeps taking the rise.”

“What would you have me do?”

“Send a small group to find out if the road is still there. Move the families away from the fields. Keep the wall ready until we know more.”

The roadwarden glanced at Aro. “Would you support that?”

“Yes,” Aro said. “As long as the wall remains prepared.”

“And if the route is blocked?”

“Then I will take the shot if it comes to that.”

The roadwarden nodded once. “That is a plan with a condition. I can work with a condition.”

“It isn’t only the marker,” Philip said. “We saw the giant choose the rise. We found its tracks turning at the old causeway. Filroy knows the dry ground past the farms.”

“The fox?”

“He’s outside the wall.”

The roadwarden considered him. “Bring him to the gate. I will speak to him if he chooses to speak to me.”

“He doesn’t take orders.”

“That may make him easier to interview than some people in this room.”

Aro gave a short, surprised laugh. The roadwarden did not smile, but her eyes softened by a fraction.

“I’ll send a roadworker,” she said. “One hour. If you find a passable route, bring back evidence. If you don’t, the city will prepare the wall.”

Father’s hands tightened on the cot blanket. “The old turn is there. I remember it.”

“You remember a map,” Philip said. “We need to see what is on the ground.”

Father looked at him, then nodded. “Yes.”

The healer turned the cot toward the door. “Now you need rest.”

“I can still help—”

“You can help by staying conscious long enough to heal.”

Father looked ready to argue, then stopped. The cot rolled out of the room.

The roadwarden handed Philip a folded map and a small brass token. “Show the token at the east service gate. The roadworker will meet you there.”

Philip took them. The map showed the current road clearly and the old road as a dotted line that ended where the farms began.

“The missing part is the problem,” he said.

“Yes,” said the roadwarden. “That is why you have one hour.”
