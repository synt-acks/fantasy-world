# Chapter Twenty-Seven

## The Long Road Around

The giant came to the outer farms at dusk.

The field walls reached no higher than its knees. It moved between them slowly, stepping over a collapsed section and turning aside from a cart abandoned near the road. Behind it, dust and rain lifted from the ground with each footfall.

The defenders formed a line near the east wall. They carried spears, crossbows, and two heavy bows mounted on frames. None of the weapons made the line look large. The roadwarden stood behind it with the gate captain and a map rolled beneath her arm.

The giant stopped where the river road met the old farm lane. Its head turned toward the city wall. The gate captain raised one hand. Aro drew his bow.

The defenders did not move as one. A crossbowman lifted his weapon too soon and had to lower it when the captain signaled. One of the heavy bows creaked as its crew shifted the frame. On the road behind them, people pushed carts toward the inner gate. A wheel struck a stone and lodged. Two guards left the line to free it, then ran back before the giant took another step.

The roadwarden repeated the evacuation order. Her voice carried only as far as the first row of workers. The gate captain sent a runner to the inner road. No one shouted at the giant. No one wanted to be the sound it turned toward.

Philip watched the evacuation line move. A cart had been turned sideways in the gate and blocked the passage. Two guards lifted one end while the owner pulled at the reins. The horse resisted, then stepped forward when a child came to its head with a handful of oats.

“Keep moving,” the gate captain called. “Leave what you can’t carry.”

One woman looked back toward the fields. Her house stood nearest the outer wall. She had locked it before leaving, though the lock would not matter to a giant. Philip wanted to tell her that the city could rebuild a house, but it would have sounded like a promise he had no right to make.

He moved closer to Hedd. “Are the workers clear?”

“Almost.”

“They need to be clear now.”

Hedd signaled again. The last two workers left the lane and moved behind the stone wall. The road stood open between the giant and the ridge.

Philip stood beside the cleared causeway, far enough from the front that Hedd could pull him back if the ground shifted. His head still hurt from the reading. The wayknife hung at his belt, heavy against his hip.

“It’s looking at the wall,” someone said.

“It’s looking past the wall,” Philip answered.

The giant turned its shoulders. Its gaze followed the line of the basalt rise. It took one step toward the wall, then another toward the eastern lane. The old route lay open but narrow, half-hidden by brush and the broken farm barrier.

“It keeps angling east,” Philip said. “It went around the wet hollow. It turned at the causeway. It’s looking for the high ground.”

“Or the city,” Aro said. His arrow remained aimed at the giant’s upper chest. “If it comes closer, I will shoot.”

Philip looked at the roadwarden. “We need the old turn open all the way. The barrier is down, but there are branches beyond the cut. It may not see the path.”

“The workers are there,” she said.

“They need to move farther back.”

Hedd signaled to the crew. They retreated behind the drainage cut, leaving only the marked lane open. One worker stumbled over the barrier and dropped a pry bar. The clatter rang across the field.

The giant turned toward it.

The worker froze. Philip saw the giant’s hand lift. Aro’s bow drew tighter. Then the giant’s gaze moved from the fallen tool to the open strip of basalt beyond it. Its hand lowered. It stepped around the worker’s position, not toward him, and the roadwarden’s guards pulled the man back by his coat.

“Clear the lane,” Philip called. “No loose tools. No one behind it.”

The roadwarden repeated the instruction. Two workers gathered the tools and moved behind the stone wall.

The giant shifted its weight. A low wall collapsed beneath its foot, sending stones into the lane. One struck the drainage slab and bounced toward the workers. Hedd pulled Philip behind the post. The stone hit the road where Philip had been standing and broke in two.

“Clear,” Philip said, though the workers were already behind cover.

The giant did not look toward him. It was watching the slope beyond the wall. Its hand pressed briefly against the earth again, testing the ground. The gesture was slow and uncertain, not a threat, though Philip knew it could become one if the path failed.

Father’s handcart stood inside the gatehouse. He had insisted on being carried to the wall, though he could not see much from where he lay. The guards had kept him behind the stone post. He did not call Philip away.

The roadwarden gave a signal. Two crews moved to the old lane and pulled the last branches clear. Another group opened the service gate wider. The gate captain ordered families from the nearest farms toward the inner road.

The giant stepped forward. A farm wall cracked beneath its heel.

Aro’s bow creaked.

“The marker,” Philip said. “There’s one at the sound post. I can check the direction once, then we open the turn.”

“You’re still dizzy,” Hedd said. “I’m here to steady you if you need it.”

Father’s voice carried from the gatehouse. “Philip, you don’t have to—”

He stopped. The order did not come.

Philip went to the sound post, a short iron-capped stone beside the old road. He knelt, set one hand against the ground, and brought the knife close. The hilt warmed. The trace pulled east, faint beneath the heavy rhythm of the giant’s steps. It was a direction, no more than that. It did not say the hollow was safe. It did not say the giant would follow.

Philip pulled the knife away.

The road leaned sideways. His stomach rolled. He caught the post and stayed still until the stones stopped shifting under him.

His fingers had gone numb around the hilt. He slipped the knife back into its sheath and pressed both palms to the marker. The metal was cool now. The ground beneath it did not move. He focused on that until his balance returned enough for him to speak.

“East,” he said. “The old line is there. Open the full turn. Keep the workers back.”

The roadwarden repeated the instruction. The crew dragged the last barrier aside and cleared the lane. Filroy stood at the far end, beyond the workers, on the dry rise. He did not call or beckon. He simply moved along the stones, staying clear of the giant’s path.

The giant stepped over the broken wall and faced the city again.

The defenders held. The heavy bows remained aimed, though no one released. Aro’s arrow tracked the giant’s shoulder as it turned. Philip could see the line of the shaft against the gray sky, thin as a pencil mark. The gate captain raised a hand to stop the archers from loosing. The giant’s next step landed beside the old farm lane, crushing a low wall but leaving the open stones clear.

Aro’s bow was still drawn. Philip saw his hands tighten on the grip. The old story stood between them: the swallowed friend, the heart cut free, the body that had not survived the victory.

“It has not attacked us,” Philip said.

“It has broken the farms.”

“It’s not trying to reach us.”

“You cannot know that.”

“No. But we can see where it keeps choosing to go.”

The giant’s head turned. Its attention shifted from the wall to the open lane. It lowered one hand and pressed the ground ahead of it. The fingers spread against the stone. It tested the route, then rose.

Aro’s arrow remained drawn for one more breath.

Then he lowered the bow.

“Open the gate,” he said.

The roadwarden ordered the service gate and the old turn kept clear. The defenders stepped back from the lane, leaving space. The giant moved forward. It stopped at the drainage cut, where water still ran through the stones. It placed one foot on the near bank, tested the firmness, and crossed.

The old road held.

The giant paused halfway across. Its foot had sunk into the wet edge, and the drainage stones shifted under its weight. Philip heard the crew suck in breath together. The giant lifted its foot, set it farther onto the broad slab, and tested again. The stone settled but did not break.

Only when it had transferred its weight did the people behind the wall begin to breathe normally.

The giant followed the basalt rise east. It did not hurry. It did not look back at the city. The crew kept far behind, clearing fallen branches only after it had passed. Aro walked with his bow unstrung. Father’s handcart followed at a distance, guarded by two soldiers.

Philip’s head still swam. Hedd offered an arm. Philip took it.

They were not leading the giant. They were making a road available and keeping people away from its feet. The difference was not large enough to make anyone safe, but it was large enough to give the giant a choice.

Ahead, the old causeway curved into the trees.
