# Chapter Three

## The Early Trip

By the time Father closed the shop, the light had gone from the square and the windows showed more lamplight than sky.

Philip carried the ledger upstairs. His father brought the day’s takings and the wrapped arrows’ empty place in the stock book. They counted the coins at the kitchen table while a pot of mushroom stew warmed over the stove. The numbers matched. They always did when Philip counted them, though Father still checked.

“Aro said you have a gift for selling,” Father said.

“He bought all twelve arrows.”

“That’s a good sale.”

“He knew exactly what he wanted.”

“Most customers don’t.”

Philip aligned the coins by size. “What did he tell you?”

Father set down the ledger. “About what?”

“The north road.”

“We spoke about Malcore.”

“That’s not what you spoke about.”

Father looked toward the window, though the road outside had gone dark. “I think we should go to Malcore.”

Philip’s hand stopped over the coins. “When?”

“Tomorrow. We leave at first light.”

The words arrived with the same ordinary calm as a request to bring down another sack of flour. Philip looked at his father, waiting for a smile or some sign that this was a joke. Father merely folded the ledger shut.

“We went last year.”

“We did.”

“We weren’t going to go again for a while.”

“Plans change.”

Philip glanced at the coins. “What’s changed?”

Father drew his chair back and sat. He looked more worn than he had when Aro left, though the day was not yet over. “I think some time away would do us good.”

“You said that last year.”

“Did I?”

“You said Hazelwood was a little full.”

“It can be.”

Philip thought of the dock, the bruises, the faces above him. Father knew about the boys. He had not brought it up again. Philip had not, either. The subject sat between them as neatly as a third place setting.

“Is this because of them?”

“It’s not only because of them.”

“That means it is partly because of them.”

“It means we have reasons to go.”

His father reached for the coins and began sorting them again. He did that when he wanted a conversation to stop. It was a useful habit in the shop, where most conversations should stop once the customer had paid.

“I like Hazelwood,” Philip said. “I like the shop. I don’t want to leave just because some boys decided I’m an easy target.”

He liked knowing which drawer held the spare weights and which customers needed to be asked twice before they admitted what they were actually looking for. He liked the steady work of balancing the till, the little satisfaction of finding a missing measure where someone had set it down, and the way Father trusted him with the keys when a delivery came after dark. The shop was not a small life. It was a life he understood.

“They don’t decide what you are.”

“Then why did you tell me to fight them?”

Father’s fingers went still against the table.

“Because I don’t want you hurt.”

“It didn’t stop them.”

“I know.”

The answer was so quiet that Philip looked up. His father’s mouth was set, but the anger had gone from it. For a moment he seemed to have run out of instructions.

“I don’t want to be a fighter,” Philip said. “I want to run the shop.”

“You can do that.”

“You keep telling me I need to be strong.”

“You do.”

“For what?”

His father did not answer. The stew simmered on the stove. From the front room came the soft tick of the clock above the shop stairs. Each sound gave the silence another place to settle.

Philip looked down at the coins. The largest was a little worn around its edge. He turned it until the stamped crown caught the lamplight.

“You never tell me what you mean,” he said.

Father rested his hands on the table. “Sometimes I don’t know how to say it without making it sound like an order.”

“You could try saying it before it turns into one.”

His father took that in without looking away. Philip could not tell whether he agreed. The answer might have been hidden in the same place as the one about the giant.

“Strength isn’t only fighting,” Father said at last.

“You said I should hit them.”

“I said you should stand your ground.”

“Those sounded like the same thing.”

“They aren’t.”

Philip waited for more. Father looked down at the ledger again. No more came.

They ate the stew while it was still warm. Philip asked how many days the trip would take. “A few,” Father said. “The wagon should make it easier than walking, if the road holds and the horses keep their pace.” They would buy stock, speak to the suppliers, and come home. It was the familiar trip: sacks, crates, lists, and a room above an inn where the floorboards complained whenever someone crossed them.

“And the giant?” Philip asked.

“We’ll hear more in Malcore.”

“From Aro?”

“From people who know the roads.”

That answer sounded sensible and incomplete. Philip was beginning to understand that the two qualities often traveled together.

After supper, Father went to the storeroom and returned with a small wooden box. He carried it in both hands, though it was no bigger than a loaf of bread. Its dark surface had been polished smooth by years of handling. The hinges were narrow and precise. A lock sat in the front, worked from pieces of metal that fitted together in a pattern Philip could not follow.

He had been gone long enough for Philip to wash the bowls and stack them beside the sink. When Father returned, the box rested across both his palms as carefully as if it might wake.

“This is dwarven,” Philip said.

“The box is.”

“What’s inside?”

“Something I was given when I left the Slayers.”

Philip touched one of the hinges. “You never said you had it.”

“I didn’t think it was time.”

“And now it is?”

Father set the box on the table. “I want you to have it.”

Philip looked from the lock to his father. “Why?”

“Because I think it’s time you had it.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

Father’s eyes narrowed a little. The words had sounded familiar because they were. Philip had used them earlier, when Father told him the giant was in the forest but could not say if it would come to Hazelwood.

Philip drew a finger along the box’s smooth lid. “Does it have anything to do with the giant?”

“Not directly.”

“Does that mean it has something to do with the road?”

Father looked at him for a long moment. “It has to do with my old work.”

“The Slayers.”

“Yes.”

“You never tell me anything about them.”

“There isn’t much that would help you.”

“You can’t know that without telling me.”

Father’s hand rested beside the box, close to the lock but not touching it. “The Slayers are gone. I don’t want you thinking this belongs to them more than it belongs to you.”

“I don’t even know what it is.”

“You will.”

“When?”

Father drew the box toward himself and then pushed it back again, a small movement that made the polished wood scrape against the table. “When I can explain it properly.”

Philip felt the old frustration rise. “You keep deciding what I’m ready to hear.”

“I’m trying to be careful.”

“You say that when you don’t want to answer.”

Father did not deny it. He lifted the box and placed it in his own travel bag, open beside him. He fitted the box between his clothes so it would not slide, then tied the flap twice. A small key appeared in his hand; he checked it, then returned it to the inside pocket of his vest.

Philip watched him. “You’re taking it with us.”

“Yes.”

“And I still won’t know why.”

“Not yet.”

The answer irritated Philip. It also confirmed that the box mattered. He had no way of knowing whether it mattered in a way that would change his life or only because Father had carried it for so long. Adults often kept objects for reasons that were clearer to the object than to anyone else.

After Father went to bed, Philip lay awake listening to the shop settle. He could hear the clock, the wind against the shutters, and his father moving once in the room across the hall. The day had started with a river and ended with a box he was not allowed to open. Between them stood seven boys, a giant in the forest, and a trip that had become urgent without becoming clear.

He packed a change of clothes, two books, his school things, and the twelve coins he had saved. Father added a small purse of household money and reminded him not to spend it on sweets. Philip had never spent household money on sweets. He had spent his own money on sweets, which was different in the way that mattered to Philip and perhaps not in the way it mattered to Father.

He made a list on the back of an old receipt: shirt, socks, book, school slate, pencil, coins. Then he checked the bag against the list and the list against the bag. The first time, he had forgotten the slate. The second time, he had written “slate” twice. The third time was correct, though it did not make the bag any larger.

He stood over the two books for several minutes. One was a history of Malcore’s old bridges, which he had already read once. The other was a collection of short accounts from travelers, most of whom had been surprised by weather. He chose the bridge book. If the weather turned out to be surprising, he could look out the window.

He left his lantern burning while he packed. He told himself it was because the room was dim. The room was dim. It was also true that he preferred to see what stood in the corners.

Father secured his travel bag and checked the wagon’s harness twice, then laid the route map beside the reins. Philip noticed a second map rolled beneath it, older and more worn. Father tucked that one out of sight when he saw Philip looking.

“Is that the route we’re taking?” Philip asked.

“It’s an old map.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“We’ll take the South Road.”

Philip waited. Father folded the newer map and put it in his coat. The older one disappeared into the travel bag, beside the box.

At the front door, Philip stopped with his hand on the latch. The rooms above the shop smelled of stew, lamp oil, and the lavender soap from the washbasin. He had never thought of them as a place he might have to miss. Father was already outside, checking the harness again.

Philip looked once toward the dark shelves below the stairs. He closed the front door and checked the bolt. Upstairs, he left the lantern burning. Sleep came late and did not stay.

Before dawn, the horses stamped in the yard, their breath white in the chill. Philip climbed onto the passenger bench with his bag between his boots. Father climbed up beside him and gathered the reins.

“Ready?” he asked.

Philip looked back at the shop windows, dark now, their shelves hidden behind the glass. “No.”

His father waited.

Philip settled the bag against his feet. “But I’m coming.”

The horses leaned into their traces. The wagon rolled south, out of Hazelwood and onto the old road.
