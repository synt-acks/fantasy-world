# Chapter Fifteen

## Stormline

The old bridge was made of stone, but the road that reached it had been made of earth.

They heard the stream before they saw the bridge. It ran fast beneath the trees, swollen from the rain that had already passed north of them. Philip stopped the stretcher on a rise and let the water’s sound help him judge where the ground fell away. The path was narrow, and the trail’s outer edge had slumped toward the cut.

“We should wait for daylight,” Filroy said.

“The rain is coming before daylight,” Philip replied.

“Then we find shelter before it reaches us.”

They searched the slope for a place above the stream. The hillside offered roots and stones but no cave. The bridge abutment was the best cover they could find, and even that depended on how much water the stream carried. Philip did not like shelter that relied on the ground remaining exactly as it was. He liked it less than walking in the rain, which was how he knew the choice was not a good one.

By the time they reached it, rain had darkened the stones and turned the slope to a slick brown path. The bridge crossed a narrow cut in the ground where a stream ran toward the Big River. Its side walls were low and worn. Roots had grown through the abutment on the east bank, gripping the old stones in a tangled lattice.

Philip set the stretcher down on the bridge approach. Father’s breathing was shallow and uneven. Rain ran through his hair and along the edge of the coat that covered him.

Philip checked Father’s face, then his hands. Father’s skin was cold. The coat was wet through at one shoulder, and the blanket beneath him had gathered water at the fold. Philip shifted it and slid a dry piece of canvas under Father’s neck.

“I’m going to get you under the bridge,” he said. “It’ll be cramped. We’ll keep your leg still.”

Father’s eyelids moved, but he did not answer.

“We should get below the bridge,” Filroy said.

Philip looked at the bank. The eastern side sloped down toward the stream. The ground there was soft, but the rooted abutment offered some shelter from the wind.

“We’ll secure the stretcher first.”

He found a length of harness webbing in the pack. It was torn but strong enough to hold a few pulls. Philip tied one end around the stretcher frame and the other around a stone post at the bridge entrance. Then he tested the knot with a hard tug.

The post did not move.

Philip leaned his weight on the line, then let it slacken. The knot held, but the iron ring shifted a fraction in its socket. He tested the surrounding stone with the heel of his hand. The ring had been fixed into the abutment, though the mortar around it was cracked. A line was only as strong as what held it. The thought came from the river dock, where the bank ring had kept him from being carried downstream. Here, he needed the stone as well as the iron to keep its promise.

He threaded the webbing through a second point around the root mass, not as a replacement for the ring but as a backup. The root was thick and partly exposed. He pulled until bark creaked. It held.

He checked the ground around it. The stones were set into the bridge, but the soil had washed away from the outer edge. He moved the knot to an iron ring fixed deeper into the abutment and tested it again. The line held.

The river rope had been useful because Philip checked where it led. The harness line held because he checked what it was tied to. He did not trust a line simply because it was there.

The rain thickened. Water began running down the road in narrow streams. Filroy disappeared beneath the bridge to find a dry place, then returned to lead Philip around the east side of the abutment.

“There’s a hollow under the roots,” he said. “It is not deep, but it is dry above the waterline.”

Philip looked at the stretcher. “Can we get him there?”

“If we move before the bank gives way.”

They lifted together. Philip took the front poles. Filroy pushed against the rear frame with his shoulder, helping keep it straight. The stretcher scraped over stone. Father made a sound and clenched one hand around the blanket.

Lightning split the sky.

The flash lit the bridge, the river, and the far bank in a single white moment. A tree on the western slope caught the strike. Its upper branches burst into flame, and the trunk cracked near its base.

The fire lasted only seconds before the rain smothered it. The broken tree leaned over the path, shedding branches. One struck the stream and spun away. Philip flinched at each crack, then forced himself to watch the bank. The land beneath them had already been softened by water. The storm did not need magic to make it dangerous.

Thunder followed. The sound rolled through the ravine and came back at them from the bridge.

The bank beneath Philip’s feet shifted.

He stopped. “Filroy—”

The earth gave way.

The stretcher lurched. The harness line snapped tight against the ring. Father slid toward the edge, held only by the canvas and the frame. Philip grabbed one pole with both hands.

The line held. The bank did not.

The webbing bit into Philip’s palms. He could hear the fibers stretching. Filroy reached for the pole, but a wash of mud cut between them. Philip’s boots slid. The stretcher tilted, canvas pulling tight across Father’s chest. For one breath Philip held on with both hands and could not tell whether he was saving Father or only keeping himself attached to the fall.

Philip was thrown downhill. His shoulder struck the slope, and the side of his head hit a root. The world spun. Mud and rain filled his mouth. He rolled until a low shelf of stone stopped him.

For several seconds he could not remember his name.

Then he heard the stream and the storm. He pushed himself upright. The bridge was above him, half-hidden by rain. The stretcher hung near the abutment, caught on the line. Father was not visible.

“Father!”

The wind took the word.

Philip reached for the wayknife. His fingers trembled as he held it near the old marker beside the path. The hilt warmed. The vibration came too quickly, too strongly. He felt movement running through the stone, a heavy rhythm that crossed and crossed again. The impression filled his head until he could not tell which way the slope lay.

He stumbled. His knee struck rock.

The knife told him nothing about Father. It did not know where he was. It did not know what the river had taken. It only held a trace of movement through the marker, stronger than the rain and louder than Philip’s own thoughts.

He dropped the knife into the mud and covered his ears, though there was no sound.

The sensation faded. His stomach rolled. He put the dagger away and pressed both hands to the ground. The earth was real. It sloped down toward the stream. The bridge was uphill.

Philip started climbing back.

The slope was steep and slick. He tested each root before trusting it. A rope of harness webbing dangled from above, but he could not see what it was attached to. He did not grab it until he had checked the line, found the iron ring, and felt the stone around it hold under his weight.

Then he climbed.

By the time he reached the bridge approach, the stretcher had shifted behind the rooted abutment. He could see the canvas through the rain, but not Father’s face. The harness line still held. The bank had broken away between them.

Philip crawled to the edge and called his father’s name.

No answer came.

Rain blurred the bridge and the trees. The stream rose below him. Somewhere under the stone, the roots creaked.

Philip could not see Father.
