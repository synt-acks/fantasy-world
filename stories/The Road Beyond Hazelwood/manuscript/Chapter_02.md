# Chapter Two

## The Arrow Seller

The afternoon belonged to Philip, more or less.

Unkati was the day Father let him run the shop. Father still came through from the storeroom when a delivery arrived or a customer asked for a price Philip had never seen in the ledger, but the counter was Philip’s, as were the sums, the stock book, and the small decisions that made a shop run smoothly. It was not a kingdom. It was better than a kingdom, because the shelves had labels.

Philip had just finished adding the flour account for the second time when the bell above the door jingled.

The man who entered wore the sort of coat people turned to look at and then pretended they had not. It was made from a beast’s hide, broad across the shoulders and dark at the edges, with a pair of folded wings hanging nearly to his boots. The creature’s narrow head had been worked into the hood. Its hooked beak rested beside the man’s ear, and the hide smelled faintly of rain, leather, and old smoke.

The man himself was lean and travel-worn. His boots had holes at the seams. His fair hair, streaked with mud, fell past his shoulders. A bow rode across his back, and a quiver sat high enough to clear the long tails of his coat.

“Do you sell arrows?” he asked.

Philip knew the voice. He had heard it at the square, in stories told by people who had never been within ten miles of the places they described. This was Aro Silvet, a famous hunter and archer south of the borderlands—or, depending on who was telling the story, the finest beast tracker, wilderness guide, and general person to have survived a remarkable number of things.

Philip stood too quickly and knocked his knee against the counter. “Yes, sir. We have arrows. They’re just over here.”

“Aro,” the man said. “Sir makes me sound like I’m about to inspect the shelves.”

Philip reached beneath the counter for the arrow box. “Aro, then.”

He placed it on the wood and lifted the lid. The arrows inside were slender and pale, each with a dark green binding below the fletching. An elf had traded them at the shop the previous season. Philip had checked every shaft before putting them away. None was bent. The points were clean and narrow, and the feathers had been cut to the same length with the kind of care that made a person suspect the maker had measured them twice and then measured the measuring tool.

Aro picked one up without touching the point. He rolled the shaft between his fingers, tested the balance, and ran his thumb along the binding.

“What would you check first?” he asked Philip.

Philip leaned across the counter. “Whether the shaft is straight.”

“And after that?”

“The fletching. If one side is heavier, it could turn.”

Aro nodded. He sighted along the shaft, then spun it lightly between two fingers. The arrow turned without wobbling. “You know the parts.”

“I sell them.”

“That is how most people learn. They think a bow does the work and the arrow merely goes along. An arrow has opinions.”

Philip studied the green binding. “Can you tell what kind of wood it is?”

“Not without cutting it open. But I can tell it has been dried properly.” Aro tapped the shaft near its center. “That matters when the weather changes. The maker knew the wood would swell, and left room for it.”

Philip had thought of arrows as things that were either straight or bent, sharp or dull. Aro seemed to see the decisions that had gone into each one. Philip wondered what a person would have to learn to notice all of them.

“Elven work?”

“That’s what the seller said.”

“And what did you say?”

“I asked him to show me the other arrows he had.”

Aro smiled. “Good answer.”

Philip felt the back of his neck warm. “They’re the finest arrows in Hazelwood.”

“I believe you.” Aro sighted along the shaft, though it was not made for sighting. “Whoever made these knew what the wood would do after it left the bow. That matters more than a polished point.”

Philip glanced at the box. He had noticed the even shafts and the clean bindings, but had not considered what they would do in flight. He wanted to ask. Aro was already putting the arrow back.

“How many?”

“All of them, if you’ll sell them.”

Philip blinked. “There are twelve.”

“I can count.”

The remark was dry, not unkind. Philip closed the lid halfway, then opened it again. A customer might ask for all twelve and buy two. That happened often. People liked to make a decision sound larger before they knew its price.

“They’re not cheap,” Philip said.

“Good.” Aro leaned on the counter. “Cheap arrows have a habit of being cheap at the important end.”

Philip named the price. He had added a little for the elven work and a little more because the arrows were rare. He had not added enough to be ashamed of it. Aro paid without bargaining, and Philip counted the coins once, then a second time because he did not want to be the sort of shopkeeper who trusted a stranger’s pile of money more than his own arithmetic.

“You’re careful,” Aro said.

“I’m counting.”

“That’s one way to be careful.”

Philip began wrapping the arrows in brown paper. He kept their points turned away from the edge of the counter. “What do you need twelve for?”

“I don’t know yet.”

Philip looked up.

Aro’s smile had disappeared. “The road has been giving me reasons to prepare.”

Before Philip could ask what that meant, Father came in from the storeroom, wiping his hands on a cloth. His attention went first to the hide coat, then to the man wearing it.

“Aro Silvet,” he said. “I wondered when you’d come through again.”

“I was beginning to think you’d stopped keeping arrows.”

“We keep what people need.”

“That must be why Philip found these.” Aro tapped the wrapped bundle. “He has a gift for selling.”

Father’s face softened with pride. “He means to stay with the shop. The wild places don’t much appeal to him.”

“A wise preference,” Aro said. “A wild place rarely has a roof when it rains.”

“You’re wearing one,” Philip said before he could stop himself.

Aro looked down at the hide. “This is a roof with poor manners.”

Father gave a brief laugh. Philip had never heard him laugh with a customer before. It made the moment feel less like a sale and more like the beginning of a story that had been waiting in the back room.

Aro looked at Philip again. “You look like a warrior.”

Philip’s fingers tightened around the paper. He thought of the dock and the river and the seven boys standing above him. “I don’t.”

“Most people don’t, until they’ve had to be one.”

“That isn’t always something to hope for,” Father said.

Aro studied him. “Your son’s name is Philip?”

“Yes.”

Something shifted in Aro’s expression. It was brief, but the shop was quiet enough for Philip to see it. The hunter glanced at the bundle, at the counter, and finally at the boy.

“I had a friend named Philip,” he said. “He was a brave man.”

The word brave settled over Philip more heavily than it should have. He imagined it was a word adults used when there was nothing simpler to say.

“What happened to him?”

Aro folded his hands on the counter. “An Alcrog swallowed him.”

“Did he know it was coming?”

“He knew it was there. We were tracking it through a ravine. It came down from the ledge above us.” Aro paused, choosing a place to set the memory. “The first warning was a shadow. We both saw it. I told him to run for the rocks. He went the other way, because I was still in the open.”

“Was that brave?”

Aro looked at him for a moment. “Yes.”

“Would it have been better if you had run?”

“Possibly.”

Philip looked at Father. His father had gone still beside the shelves. Aro’s answer seemed to surprise him as much as it surprised Philip.

“I don’t tell the story because it was a good plan,” Aro continued. “I tell it because he was my friend, and he did what he could with the time he had.”

Philip felt his eyes move to the hide coat. The creature’s beak rested beside Aro’s ear. Beneath it, the great wings swept down behind him, nearly brushing the floor. The hide had been scraped and stitched, but the shape of the beast remained. It was not a costume. It was what was left after the hunter had finished with the living thing.

“It swallowed him?” Philip asked.

“Whole.”

“And he got out?”

Aro’s eyes held his. “He killed it from the inside. Cut its heart out.”

Father’s cloth stopped moving in his hands.

“How?” Philip asked.

“With a blade. He had one with him.” Aro looked down at his own hands. “The Alcrog took flight. It was too late to stop it before it rose. When it fell, Philip survived the impact. He had done the impossible part. I thought I could save him after.”

He paused. No one in the shop moved. Even the bell above the door seemed to have remembered its manners.

“But he was badly wounded,” Aro continued. “He bled out before I could get him to shelter.”

Philip looked at the wrapped arrows. He had expected a story about a hero and a monster, with the kind of ending people told when they wanted children to sleep without imagining teeth. Instead, Aro had described a man who won and died anyway.

“I’m sorry,” Philip said.

Aro nodded. For the first time since he had entered, he seemed uncertain what to do with his hands. “He was my friend. He was also the sort who would have been irritated if I made too much of it.”

“Would he have wanted you to wear the hide?” Philip asked.

Father’s eyes flicked toward him. Aro did not seem offended. He looked at the coat’s winged shoulders, the creature’s beak, and the worn stitching along the hem.

“He would have asked whether it was warm,” Aro said. “And then he would have told me I’d put it on backward.”

The answer made Philip smile, though Aro did not. His gaze had turned distant, fixed on something beyond the shop wall.

Father stepped closer to the counter. Aro’s expression changed. “Not here,” he said.

Father answered in Elvish.

Philip knew a handful of words, mostly the sort travelers used when they wanted to buy something or complain about the price. His father’s speech was too quick for him. Aro replied just as quietly. They spoke with the ease of people who had once said far more to each other than the present conversation required.

Philip caught a word that sounded like road. Then another that might have meant broken. His father asked a question. Aro shook his head.

They had not moved far from the counter. That made the quiet feel deliberate. Philip busied himself with the wrapping paper, though the bundle was already tied.

When Father switched back to the common tongue, his voice was level. “You’ll go on to Malcore?”

“I’ll reach the Road Office before the week is out. They need to hear what I saw.”

“I’ll bring my family south.”

Philip looked up. His father was watching Aro, not him.

“You were planning to come later,” Aro said.

“Plans change.”

“They usually do when roads are involved.”

Father asked one more question in Elvish. Aro replied, and this time he looked directly at Philip. Whatever he had said, it was not meant for the boy.

Aro picked up the arrows. “Thank you for the sale, Philip.”

“You’re welcome.”

“Keep your eyes open on the road.”

Father’s mouth tightened slightly. “He knows how to watch where he’s going.”

Aro did not argue. He drew the hide around his shoulders and pushed the door open. The afternoon air stirred the arrow feathers through the paper. The bell gave its tired jingle as he left.

Philip watched the coat’s wings disappear past the window.

“What did you say to him?” he asked.

Father gathered the coins and returned them to the till. “We talked about the north road.”

“In Elvish?”

“It’s a useful language for private conversation.”

That was not an explanation. Philip looked at the coins, at the empty space where the arrows had been, and at his father’s hands as they closed the till. Father counted the money once. Then, after a pause, he counted it again.

The numbers came out the same both times.
