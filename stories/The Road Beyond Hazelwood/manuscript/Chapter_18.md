# Chapter Eighteen

## The False Signal

Aro found them in the afternoon where the road climbed above the stream.

Philip heard him before he saw him: the scrape of a boot on loose stone, followed by a pause that lasted long enough to mean someone was listening. Philip lifted one hand to stop Aro and Filroy. The stretcher rested against a low bank. Father was asleep. The brush ahead hid the path around the bend.

“Who is it?” Philip called.

“Aro Silvet.”

“How do I know?”

“You sold me twelve arrows.”

“That proves you were at the shop.”

“It proves I paid for the arrows. I have the receipt if you want to audit me.”

Philip glanced at Filroy. The fox’s ears were turned toward the voice. “It sounds like him.”

“It sounds like someone who wants us to think it sounds like him,” Filroy said.

Philip kept his hand raised. “Come around slowly.”

He came through the trees without his hide hood, carrying his bow in one hand and a short pack over his shoulder. The Alcrog coat was dark with rain. One of its folded wings had caught on a branch and hung loose behind him.

“You look worse than the road,” he said.

“The road has had fewer chances to sleep,” Philip replied.

Aro’s eyebrows lifted. “You’ve been talking to the fox.”

“I can hear you,” Filroy said from the rock beside the stretcher.

“I assumed.” Aro put down his pack. “I’m glad you’re alive. Both of you.”

He unbuckled the pack and took out a small bundle of food, a flask, and a dry strip of cloth. When Philip admitted they had almost lost the stretcher in the swamp, Aro gave Father a few mouthfuls of water, then handed the rest to Philip.

“We don’t need to finish it now,” Aro said. “We need enough for the next stretch.”

Philip stared at the bread. “How long have you been carrying this?”

“Since yesterday.”

“You were ahead of us.”

“I was on the road. You were in the forest.”

“You could have met us sooner.”

“I could have taken the low track and missed you entirely.” Aro folded the empty wrapping. “I reached the Shelf Road after the wagon went over. I found the wreck and the tracks leading south. The stretcher had left a line through the mud that was easier to follow than any road marker. I followed it as far as the swamp. After that, I had to wait for the water to fall.”

“You went back to the wreck?” Father asked.

“I checked the wagon first. It was burning. I couldn’t climb down safely, and I saw the trail you’d taken.”

“You could have reached us sooner if you’d gone through the low ground.”

“The low ground was under water.”

Father closed his eyes. “Right.”

Aro looked at Philip. “I should have come to the wreck sooner.”

“You wouldn’t have known we were there.”

“I knew someone had survived. There were prints.”

“We had already gone.” Philip looked at the bread and the flask. “You came when you could.”

Aro nodded, though the answer did not seem to absolve him. He was used to arriving at a problem before it got worse. This one had changed shape while he was on the road.

He covered his relief by examining the splint and the stretcher. He asked how Father had been hurt, how long they had been on the road, what water they had found, and whether the storm had damaged the old bridge. He listened to the answers without interrupting.

When he learned that Father could not walk, Aro looked toward the south. “We can move faster if we leave the stretcher and carry him between us.”

“No,” Philip said.

Aro glanced at him. “You have a better plan?”

“The stretcher keeps his leg still. Carrying him would pull it around.”

“The south road will take another day at this pace.”

“Then we’ll take another day.”

The answer had come too quickly. Philip looked at Father. The old version of him would have waited for Father to agree or had to prove he deserved to speak. He tried again.

“I don’t know if the stretcher can hold up that long,” he said. “But we can repair the frame and check the lashings. If the road gets worse, we stop.”

Father nodded once. “That’s sound.”

Aro looked between them, then accepted the point. “Very well. We’ll reinforce it.”

Before they lifted the stretcher, Aro asked Philip to show him the worst of the frame. Philip showed him the split pole and the canvas seam that had begun to fray. Aro did not replace the whole frame. He added one brace, then let Philip test it. The repair held under the weight of the stretcher, but Philip kept his hand on the lashing until the first few steps were done.

They worked together. Aro cut a straight branch from a fallen limb and lashed it beneath the stretcher’s cracked side rail. Philip checked the knots. Filroy inspected the nearby ground and disliked the way the slope narrowed before the next marker. Father watched, offering directions in short bursts when the pain allowed.

At the marker, Aro stopped.

It was a squat post of dark stone with a band of worked iron around its middle. The band had been bent outward, but the face remained intact. Aro looked at the wayknife at Philip’s belt. “May I?”

Philip hesitated, then unfastened the sheath and handed it over. Aro brought the blade within reach of the iron. The hilt warmed. A pulse traveled along the blade, and Aro’s wrist tipped toward the south road.

“The strongest trace runs straight to Malcore,” Aro said.

He returned the wayknife. Philip took it, then looked at the ground beneath the marker. Broad impressions crossed the road and angled toward the river. The deepest had filled with water; a branch lay crushed at its edge. “It doesn’t look straight.”

Aro glanced back. “The marker is clear.”

“The trace is strong. That doesn’t mean it’s the old road.”

“It is a direction.”

“It might be the giant.”

Aro rested one hand on the marker. “That is what we are trying to follow.”

“Then why does it point toward the city?”

“Because the city is south.”

“Or because the giant walked there.”

For a moment the three of them stood around the marker, listening to the rainwater drip from the trees. Aro’s confidence had not disappeared, but it had become something he was holding rather than something that held him.

“The old route bent east,” Father said. “There was a causeway before the farms expanded.”

“You remember it?”

“I remember a map. I have not walked it in years.”

Aro looked at the road, then at the bent iron. “The Road Office will need more than a boy’s guess.”

“It’s an observation,” Philip said. “I don’t know where it goes yet.”

His fingers found the knife’s hilt. He wanted to take it out, test the marker himself, and prove he had heard what he heard. He also remembered the slope spinning beneath the storm and the way the echo had filled his head until he could not tell which way was up.

He left it sheathed.

“We can compare the next marker,” he said. “And look at the tracks between them. If they match, that gives us more to work with.”

Aro studied him. “You are asking me to wait.”

“I’m asking us to check.”

Father shifted under the blanket. “Philip is right about the reading. It tells us what passed most often. It does not say why.”

Aro’s jaw tightened. “I know what a wayknife does.”

“Then you know it could be wrong for the question we’re asking.”

The sentence landed harder than Philip meant it to. Father turned his face away. Aro looked back at the marker.

“We will compare the next one,” Aro said at last. “Then we take the route that gets your father medical care.”

“Agreed.”

They continued south at a careful pace. The next marker stood beside a drainage cut, nearly hidden beneath fern. Its iron band had rusted through on one side. Aro examined it without using the wayknife. Philip kept the blade sheathed; the bent iron and rust might only confuse the trace.

They found deep impressions pressed into the earth between the posts. The tracks crossed the road, then angled toward the river. One had filled with water. A branch lay crushed beneath a print broader than Philip’s shoulders.

“It is moving along the ridge,” Filroy said. “The ground is higher there.”

Aro looked north, toward the dark line of the river. “If it reaches the river road, it will be headed for Malcore.”

“Or following the dry ground,” Philip said.

“Either way, the city is in its path.”

No one had an answer that made the road less dangerous. Father’s stretcher creaked when Aro and Philip lifted it over the drainage cut. The rain softened to a mist, but the sky remained low and gray. Somewhere beyond the ridge, a deep, slow impact shuddered through the ground.

The giant was moving again.
