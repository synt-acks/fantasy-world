# Chapter Twenty-Two

## Malcore’s Gate

Malcore’s outer gate was closed when they reached it.

The wall rose above the road in blocks of pale stone. People had gathered outside: farmers with carts, drovers with pack animals, and families carrying whatever they could lift. A guard stood on the inner parapet calling names and directions. The main gate remained shut while a narrow wicket opened and closed one person at a time.

The line advanced only when the wicket opened. A woman carried a sleeping child wrapped in a rug; an old man held the reins of a donkey that kept trying to eat the rug. A pair of farmers argued over whose cart had been there first. Neither seemed to know what they would do if the other agreed. Beyond them, the road curved east toward the farms. The warning bell had stopped, but people continued to look that way.

The gate staff were not being cruel. They were trying to keep a frightened crowd from pushing through a narrow opening at once. That still left a stretcher outside the wall, and the gate did not care what good reasons a person had.

The crowd had the anxious look of people who had heard a danger was coming but had not yet agreed what kind. Some stared east. Others watched the guards. A child sat on a sack of grain and asked for water. His mother answered that they would find some inside. She said it with the confidence of someone who needed him to stop asking.

Philip and Aro carried Father’s stretcher to the side of the gate. Filroy stayed back beneath a cart, out of the crush. His tail brushed the wheel once, then tucked away.

“We need a healer,” Philip told the guard at the wicket. “My father has a head injury and a broken leg.”

The guard leaned out. “We’re taking the injured through. Name and home?”

“Philip. Hazelwood.”

“And the fox?”

“He’s with me.”

“Foxes don’t come through the gate.”

“He’s not going to the healer.”

“That was not the part I was worried about.”

The guard looked past Philip at the stretcher. A second guard approached from inside, carrying a ring of keys. “The wicket latch is jammed again. We can’t open it wide enough for a litter.”

“Then use the main gate,” someone called from the crowd.

“The main gate is holding the crowd outside,” the guard answered. “If we open both, we will have the crowd in two places.”

Philip listened to the argument behind him. Aro shifted the stretcher to keep Father’s head raised. The canvas had grown damp again, and Father’s breath sounded rough. Philip’s own legs had begun to shake. He tried to focus on the latch, the iron pin, the groove, and the pressure of the gate against the stone. Problems with visible parts were easier to look at than the ones that could not be fixed with a tool.

Philip studied the wicket. Its iron latch slid into a groove cut into the stone. A narrow pin held the mechanism in place. When the gate shifted under pressure, the pin had slipped sideways and caught against a worn lip. The guard tried to pull it back with the key, but the key’s teeth scraped the face of the lock.

“Can I look?” Philip asked.

The guard gave him a doubtful glance. “You know gates?”

“I know when a thing is caught on the wrong side of a groove.”

It was a shop answer, not a gate answer. The guard stepped aside anyway.

Philip knelt. The iron pin was bent slightly, but not enough to break. He pressed the wicket inward with one shoulder, relieving the pressure on the latch. The pin shifted. He took the flat end of a small tool from Aro’s kit and eased it back along the groove.

“Now pull,” he said.

The guard drew the latch. The wicket swung wider.

The guard pulled once more to make sure it would stay open. The pin slid along the groove and stopped before catching. Philip watched it hold under the weight of the door, then stepped aside to let the porters take the stretcher.

“There,” Philip said. “It was holding against the stone.”

“We have a gatewright,” the guard said.

“Then you can tell him it’s holding against the stone.”

The guard almost smiled. He signaled for two porters, who came through the wicket and helped carry Father into the city. Philip stepped after them. He looked back once.

Filroy had moved from beneath the cart. He stood at the boundary stone, on the outer side of the wall. The fox was watching Philip, not the city.

“I’ll come back,” Philip said.

“You have somewhere to be,” Filroy replied.

Philip followed the stretcher inside.

The infirmary was a long stone building near the gate. The healer and her assistants took Father from them before Philip had finished explaining what had happened. They cut away his trouser leg, checked the splint, and asked how long he had been unconscious. Philip answered what he knew. Aro gave the details Philip did not have.

The healer examined Father’s eyes, cleaned the head wound, and pressed along the lower leg. Father woke and cried out when she reached the break. She told him not to move, then ordered a second assistant to bring a proper splint.

The assistants asked Philip to wait outside the curtain while they removed the soaked canvas and checked Father’s ribs. He stood in the infirmary passage with his hands empty. Aro went to speak to the gate guards. For the first time since the crash, no one needed Philip to hold a pole, tie a knot, or count breaths. He did not know what to do with his hands.

He washed them at a basin. The water ran pink, then clear. He kept rubbing after the blood was gone, until the healer called his name and told him the skin would not come back cleaner by force.

“Is he going to be all right?” Philip asked.

“He is alive, and he is in the right place,” the healer said. “That is what I can promise you now. The bone is broken. His head needs watching. He will not be walking today.”

“How long?”

“Longer than you want.”

Philip nodded. He had asked because he wanted a number. The healer had given him a truth.

Aro stood near the doorway, still wearing the wet Alcrog hide. “I need to speak to the Road Office.”

“They know the giant is coming,” the healer said. “The bell has rung.”

“Then they need the route evidence.”

Philip looked toward the window. Beyond the infirmary yard, the wall blocked the road. Beyond the wall, Filroy was somewhere among the waiting carts.

“I need to talk to the roadwarden too,” Philip said.

Aro’s gaze shifted toward him. “You’re staying with your father.”

“He’s with the healer. I can help with the road.”

“You are hurt.”

“So is he. I can still talk.”

The healer drew a curtain around Father’s bed. “You can talk for a short while. Then you will sit down, drink water, and let me look at that head wound.”

Philip agreed. It was not a battle worth having.
