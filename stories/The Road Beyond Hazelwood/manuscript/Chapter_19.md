# Chapter Nineteen

## The North Giant

They reached the ridge before the giant did.

The old road climbed between two outcrops and bent east. From the top, Philip could see the river below, brown and broad beneath the clouded sky. Beyond it lay the south road to Malcore. A line of broken trees marked the giant’s passage along the slope, sometimes hidden by the pines, sometimes visible as a raw strip of earth.

Filroy found a hollow between the rocks where the stretcher could be set down. A low shelf overhung it, enough to keep the worst of the drizzle off Father. The ridge offered a view of the trail below, but the stones screened them from the open ground.

“We wait here,” Aro said. He crouched beside the rock and eased his bow from his shoulder.

“For what?” Philip asked.

“To see where it goes.”

“You said the city is in its path.”

“I said the city may be.” Aro checked the bowstring for water. “We need to know what it does when the route is open.”

“And if it turns toward us?”

“Then we leave.”

“With Father on the stretcher?”

Aro did not answer immediately. His eyes went to the frame, then the narrow ledge above the river. “Then we hope it does not.”

It was not much of a plan, but at least it admitted the part that was missing.

Philip helped Aro ease the stretcher into the hollow. They had to lift it over a lip of stone, and Philip’s injured hand began to shake before they had cleared it. He kept his eyes on the frame instead of Father’s leg. The leg was not straight in the way Philip wished it were, and looking did not improve it.

Filroy circled the hollow twice. He sniffed the wind, then the ground, then the narrow gap between two rocks. “If we need to leave, go through there. It opens on the back side of the ridge.”

“Can we get Father through?”

“Not quickly.”

“Can we get him through at all?”

Filroy stared at the stretcher. “If you do not insist on carrying the rocks too.”

Philip crouched and checked the canvas ties. “We’ll see if we need to.”

He wanted to ask why Filroy had stayed with them. The fox had not promised to help in a fight, and Philip would not have asked him to. He was a small animal with a territory to protect and no reason to be crushed because a human boy was afraid. Philip decided not to ask for a promise he had no right to expect.

They pulled the stretcher deeper beneath the rock shelf. Philip tucked the canvas around Father and checked the splint. The swelling had not worsened, though Father’s face was pale and damp. He was awake, following the sound of their voices.

“It’s coming from the north,” Father said.

“We can see the corridor,” Philip replied.

“No. Listen.”

The ridge carried sound strangely. The river’s rush faded. Rain tapped the leaves. Then the ground gave a low, uneven shudder. Another followed after several breaths.

The giant appeared below the ridge, moving between the trees.

Its approach announced itself in the animals first. A line of birds lifted from the pines. A hare darted across the exposed slope, then stopped at the rocks below them, confused by the scent of human sweat and blood. It veered away. The giant’s weight came after: a dull thump, a pause, then another. Philip felt each one through his knees.

He counted without meaning to. Four impacts crossed the hollow. A fifth came closer than the others. The giant was not walking straight toward them; the sound moved along the ridge below, rising and falling with the slope.

In daylight, Philip could see more than he had from the hill. The giant’s head was stone-gray and blunt, fissured like a boulder left through too many winters. A heavy cloak of hide and woven branches covered its shoulders. It leaned forward as it walked, one hand touching tree trunks or rocks as if testing the ground. Its steps were slow, but each one pressed the soil into a dark hollow.

It reached a patch of brush near a leaning pine and stopped. A fox den opened at the base of the tree. A smaller fox darted from the roots and vanished into the rocks.

The giant turned its head toward the den. Philip’s breath caught. Its arm lifted.

Then it lowered the arm and stepped around the tree.

“It saw the den,” Philip whispered.

“It saw the tree,” Aro said. He had his bow raised, an arrow set against the string. “We don’t know what it saw.”

The giant continued along the ridge. Its path angled away from the river’s flooded edge, toward higher ground. When the trail dipped into a wet hollow, it stopped, shifted its weight, and climbed the stony rise instead.

“It keeps to the dry ground,” Filroy said.

“It may simply be easier to walk there,” Aro murmured.

“Those can both be true,” Philip said.

Father’s hand closed around the canvas. “The old route followed the basalt rise.”

Philip looked at him. “To Stillstone Hollow?”

“I don’t know. The map showed an eastward road. There was a shelter somewhere beyond the farms.”

Aro’s bow remained drawn. “The route bends toward the city wall.”

“At the next low ground,” Father said. “It would have had to turn east before the farms.”

The giant reached the dip where the ridge narrowed. Aro’s pack shifted against the stone beside him. A metal clasp struck the rock with a bright clink.

The giant stopped.

Its head swung toward the sound. Its shoulders turned. One hand grasped a dead pine limb lying across the trail and lifted it. The limb was thicker than a wagon axle. The giant swept it across the slope.

The branch struck the rocks beneath their shelf. Stone chips burst upward. Aro ducked. Philip threw himself over Father’s stretcher. Filroy vanished into a crack in the rock. The limb scraped the shelf’s edge, then fell down the ridge and crashed through the trees.

No one moved.

The hand that held the branch had dropped to its side. The giant’s fingers flexed once. One of its shoulders rose and fell beneath the rough covering. Philip wondered if it was breathing hard. He could hear his own breath too loudly to tell.

The giant stood facing them. Its head tilted, listening. It did not roar or call. The silence gave Philip no clue whether it was angry, frightened, or only trying to find the noise.

Philip kept his hand over Father’s shoulder. His palm felt Father’s body rise and fall beneath the blanket. The giant’s gaze passed over the rocks. It could not see into the hollow, or perhaps it did and could not tell what the hollow contained.

After a long moment, it turned away. It set one foot carefully on the dry rise and continued east.

Aro lowered the bow only after the giant disappeared behind the trees.

“That thing is dangerous,” he said.

“Yes,” Philip said.

“It can break the wall.”

“Yes.”

“And you think it is looking for a place to sleep?”

“I think it avoids the water and keeps choosing the higher ground. I don’t know why.”

Aro’s expression tightened, but not with anger. “You are right to leave room for what we don’t know.”

It was not agreement. It was a first step toward it.

“If it reaches the city, we may not get another chance,” Aro said.

“If it turns east, we may not need one,” Philip replied.

“And if it doesn’t?”

“Then we’ll know more than we know now.”

Aro looked at him. “That is not enough to protect a city.”

“It’s enough to decide what we check next.”

Father pointed with two fingers toward a low stone half-hidden beneath the roots at the east end of the ridge. “That marker is older than the road. If it is intact, it may show the turn.”

Philip looked at the iron band on his knife. He could feel a faint warmth, though he had not drawn it. The giant’s passage had disturbed the markers all around them. He wanted to know what the stone held. His head still swam from the storm, and the ledge had just been struck by a tree limb.

“We’ll check it after we’re off this ridge,” he said.

Aro glanced at him. “Not now?”

“Not while we’re hiding from the thing that made the ground shake.”

Father let out a breath that might have been the beginning of a laugh, though it hurt him. “A sensible delay.”

The ridge below them was empty. The old marker remained where it had stood, half buried and waiting. Philip had begun to understand that waiting could be a decision too.
