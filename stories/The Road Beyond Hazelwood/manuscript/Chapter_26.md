# Chapter Twenty-Six

## Broken Stones

The east service road filled with workers before the afternoon was over.

They came from the gatehouse, the farms, and the road office. Some carried shovels. Others brought pry bars, ropes, and a handcart loaded with tools. The roadwarden had sent a small crew, but the warning bells had persuaded several more people to join them. It is difficult to keep a crew small when everyone can hear the reason for the work.

The crew did not agree on what the work was for. One farmer thought they were building a barricade. A gate guard thought they were clearing a retreat path. Hedd told them they were opening an old road and would decide how to use it when they had seen what lay beyond it.

“That sounds like a good way to get halfway through two jobs,” the farmer said.

“It is,” Hedd replied. “So we will start with the ditch.”

Philip helped carry the lighter tools, though the healer had made him promise not to lift anything heavy. A woman with a red scarf took the pry bar from him and handed him a coil of marking cord instead. He started to object, then noticed his fingers had begun to tremble around the rope. He thanked her and carried the cord.

Hedd directed the clearing at the drainage cut. Philip stayed behind the first line of workers as promised. Aro watched the ridge with his bow unstrung. Filroy moved along the edge of the old lane, then returned to the basalt rise.

One of the farmers whose land touched the old lane stood by the gate, refusing to leave his cart. He had loaded it with sacks and a wooden chest. The cart’s wheel had sunk into the soft shoulder, and he was trying to free it by pulling the horse sideways.

“The roadwarden said to move inside,” Hedd called.

“I’m not leaving my tools out here.”

“You can bring the cart through the service gate.”

“The wheel is stuck.”

Philip looked at the ground. The wheel had sunk into the edge of the drainage ditch. The horse was pulling at an angle, making the rut deeper. He walked over, staying inside the marked cord.

“Can you unhitch the horse?” he asked.

The farmer looked at him. “What would that do?”

“It would stop the cart from pulling deeper while we lift the wheel.”

The farmer hesitated, then let the reins go. Hedd and two workers levered the axle while Philip pushed a flat stone beneath the wheel. It rose out of the rut. The horse was led through the gate, then the cart followed.

The farmer picked up his tools and went inside without thanking anyone. He had remembered the sack of seed on the cart and was counting it under his breath. Philip understood. Some people used thanks when they had something left to spare.

“The dry ground continues,” he said. “The thorns are thick, but the stones are under them.”

Hedd and two workers levered the wooden barrier out of its brackets. It scraped free and toppled onto the grass. They dragged it aside, then cleared the loose branches from the ditch. Water trickled through the opened gap. The old stones held.

The flow increased as the blockage loosened. Silt poured out in a brown ribbon. One of the workers stepped too close and the turf slumped beneath his boot. Hedd caught the back of his coat and pulled him away. They drove a stake into firm ground and tied the marking cord across the soft edge.

Philip watched the little stream cut through the mud. “If it keeps draining, the bank might hold.”

“Might,” Hedd said. “We’ll put a cart over it before we ask a giant.”

The water carried out a branch and a strip of rotten fence. Hedd set a second worker to clear the far side while the first kept watch on the bank. They used a rope to move the heavier stones, lifting one at a time and testing each place before putting weight on it. The work went slowly. Whenever someone suggested moving faster, Hedd asked which part of the ditch they wanted to collapse.

No one answered.

“The map says the turn is farther west,” Hedd called.

Philip compared the dotted line with the exposed stonework. “The map is off. The road bends here, along the ridge.”

“You’re sure?”

“No. The tracks and the ground say it does. The marker said there was an older line east, but it was faint.”

Hedd looked at the map, then at the ridge. He scratched out the old line and drew a new one. “I’ll mark it as observed, not confirmed.”

“That’s fair.”

The roadwarden arrived with two gate guards. She checked the cleared ditch, examined the stone turn, and spoke with Hedd. Then she looked to Philip.

“Can the wayknife confirm the route here?”

“There’s a marker at the sound post. I could try it, but my head is still moving from the last reading.”

The roadwarden looked from the marker to the open turn. “Do we need another reading to clear the road?”

Philip studied the tracks in the soft bank and the fitted stones under the grass. “The causeway trace ran east. The ground and the prints show the turn. We can clear it far enough to test the road without pretending we know where it ends.”

“Then we’ll mark it as a working route,” she said. “No one calls it confirmed until the far side is inspected.”

Philip let the wayknife stay sheathed. He could feel the urge to ask it again, to turn a direction into an answer. The dizziness had not passed, and the marker could not tell them whether the giant would choose the road. The stones and tracks were the evidence they had.

The roadwarden wrote the observation on the survey sheet. She did not ask Philip to read again.

Father had been brought to the outer gate on a handcart, against the healer’s better judgment and with a guard assigned to watch him. He could not stand. His leg was bound in the proper splint, and the cot was secured to the cart. He called out from the shelter of the gatehouse.

“The causeway should meet the east farm lane beyond the second well!”

Hedd checked the map. “There are three wells.”

“The one with the stone trough,” Father said. “It was there when I last walked the route.”

“That narrows it down,” Philip said.

Father gave him a tired look. “It was a memorable trough.”

“I’m sure.”

The roadwarden sent a pair of workers to check the lane. The rest continued clearing the stones. They uncovered a broad stretch of the old turn and enough of the drainage channel to let water escape. A farm cart crossed the cut without sinking. No one called that proof a giant could cross, but it was the first useful test.

The cart’s wheels left two shallow ruts on the far bank. The roadwarden had the crew mark them and measure the depth. Hedd checked the axle clearance and the stones under the water. The cut had passed one ordinary test. The giant would weigh far more than a cart, but the test told them where the ground held and where it did not.

“We should put another stone here,” Philip said, pointing to the softer edge.

Hedd shook his head. “A loose stone will roll under a heavy foot. We need a broad bed, or the weight goes into the bank.”

They fetched a slab from the broken barrier and laid it across the shallowest part. Hedd tested it with a pry bar. It shifted by less than a finger’s width. He marked that too.

A bell rang from the wall.

The giant had reached the outer approach.

Aro strung his bow. He moved to a position where he could see through a gap in the trees. His face settled into the expression Philip remembered from the shop, when Aro had talked about the Alcrog. Only now Philip knew that confidence and grief could wear the same coat.

“Hold the road clear,” the roadwarden told the workers. “Move everyone back from the approach. No one fires unless it reaches the wall.”

Aro raised his bow.

The giant emerged beyond the fields.
