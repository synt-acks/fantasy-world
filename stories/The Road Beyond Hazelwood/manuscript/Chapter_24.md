# Chapter Twenty-Four

## The Buried Turn

The east service gate stood behind a row of storehouses.

It was narrower than the main gate and opened onto a track bordered by garden walls. A roadworker waited beside it with a shovel over one shoulder and a rolled survey sheet beneath his arm. He introduced himself as Hedd, then looked at Philip’s bruised hand and the fresh wrap around his head.

“You’re the marker reader?”

“I’m the one who saw the tracks.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No.”

Hedd seemed satisfied by the answer. He opened the gate and let them through. Aro joined them with his bow. Filroy emerged from the shadow of a cart and crossed the boundary stone without comment.

The old road began as a line of stones beneath grass. It passed behind the last gardens and reached a low field where a wooden barrier had been built across it. Beyond the barrier lay an overgrown lane. A drainage cut split the land in two, its sides lined with old stonework.

The cut was clogged with leaves, branches, and silt. Water had carved a narrow channel beneath the blockage, but the stones remained in place.

Hedd set his shovel against the near bank and leaned on it. The handle sank a little into the soft earth. Philip could see where water had undercut the grass. The old road stones were solid at the top, but the edge of the cut was not.

“If we stand there, it’ll give,” Philip said.

Hedd shifted back. “You see that from here?”

“The grass is hanging over empty space. There’s water under it.”

The roadworker tested the ground farther inland. His boot pressed down; the earth held. “Good. We’ll approach from this side.”

Philip took the branch he had been using as a walking stick and pushed it into the bank. It went through the turf, then met packed soil. He tried closer to the cut. The branch sank to its full length.

“The road stone is holding,” Hedd said, “but the bank beside it isn’t. We’ll clear the water first. No one puts weight on that edge.”

Philip liked having someone else decide where not to stand. He had been doing too much of that alone.

“This is the break,” Hedd said. “The road office marked it impassable twenty years ago.”

“Could it be cleared?” Philip asked.

“For carts, perhaps. For a giant, I don’t know.”

Filroy walked along the upper edge of the cut, sniffing at the ground. He stopped at a narrow strip of exposed basalt. “The dry rise continues there. It goes east, then bends toward a hollow.”

“You’ve been there?” Philip asked.

“I have been on the rise. I have not been in your hollow.”

Hedd unrolled his survey sheet. The dotted road ended at the drainage cut. “The old map shows a turn on the far side, but the landowner built a fence over it.”

Philip looked at the wooden barrier. It had been made to keep sheep from wandering into the gardens. Its posts were wedged between the old stones, not sunk into the earth. He tried one. It shifted.

“This could move,” he said.

“Not by one person,” Hedd replied.

“I know.”

They examined the old stones beyond the fence. One had a shallow iron groove along its top. Philip recognized the cut from the marker at the causeway. It was not a route marker, exactly, but a guide for a gate or movable barrier. The iron had rusted away, leaving the groove filled with soil.

“The turn is under here,” he said.

Hedd knelt beside him. They cleared moss and dirt with the edge of the shovel. A line of fitted stone emerged, curving east beneath the bramble.

Aro looked toward the city wall. “If the giant is following the ridge, this is where it would have turned.”

“If the way is open,” Philip said.

Hedd probed the drainage cut with his shovel. The blade struck stone. He cleared away a branch and pulled out a mat of roots. Water began to drain through the gap, slow but steady.

“We can open this enough for people to cross,” he said. “The road needs a crew. We need to move the barrier and test the far side. It may still be blocked.”

Hedd paced the far edge and looked through the bramble. A line of old stone showed beneath the roots, then disappeared into a thicket. The lane beyond was too narrow for a cart in its present state.

“It’ll take hours to clear this,” he said. “More if the far side has another washout.”

“The giant may arrive before then,” Aro said.

“Then we clear what we can and move people first,” Hedd replied. “A road that is half open is still a road for walking away from.”

Philip looked at the track marks near the barrier. The giant had stepped close enough to press a deep print into the damp earth. From the print, a smaller line of disturbed soil turned toward the east rise. It could be the creature’s attempt to find a way around the fence. It could be an ordinary change of footing. Philip refused to call it proof.

“We should mark the place it turned,” he said. “If it comes back, we can see whether it chooses the same ground.”

Hedd pulled a strip of survey cloth from his pack and tied it to a branch beyond the print. “There. If we need to compare, we can find it again.”

“Can a giant cross?” Aro asked.

“I don’t know.”

Philip looked at the footprints in the soft earth. One deep print angled toward the barrier. Another had turned along the slope, where the ground rose toward the east. The giant had not gone through. The path had been closed.

“We can’t promise it will take the route,” Philip said. “But we can make the route possible.”

Hedd nodded. “That I can report.”

They returned to the road office with the survey sheet marked, a strip of fitted stone exposed, and mud on their clothes. The roadwarden listened while Hedd explained the drainage cut and the movable barrier. She asked Aro to describe the giant’s direction, then asked Philip to repeat exactly what the wayknife had shown.

He did not call it a map. He did not say the giant was searching for shelter. He said what he had seen and what he believed the evidence might mean.

The roadwarden studied the marked sheet. “I can authorize a small crew to clear the barrier and open the drainage. We will move the outer farms away from the road. The city keeps the wall prepared until the giant turns.”

She asked Hedd how many people he needed. He named four for the drainage, two for the barrier, and one to watch the ridge. The roadwarden took a pencil and changed the number twice. Every worker sent to the road was someone not carrying water, moving carts, or guarding a gate. She did not make the arithmetic look easier than it was.

“We clear the drainage first,” she said. “If the water rises, we stop. The farms nearest the lane move inside the wall. No one goes beyond the marked line without a guard.”

“And the giant?” Aro asked.

“We watch it. If it reaches the wall, we act.”

“That’s reasonable,” Philip said.

“It is expensive,” the roadwarden replied. “Reasonable decisions often are.”

Father waited in the infirmary when Philip returned. He had been carried back to bed and had slept. His face was pale, his eyes tired.

“They found the turn?” he asked.

“Part of it. The crew can clear it. We don’t know if the giant will take it.”

Father’s gaze dropped to the dagger at Philip’s belt. “Then you should stay here.”

Philip felt the old argument rising between them. Father had said it softly. It still sounded like an order.

“We’ll talk after you’ve rested,” Philip said.

“Philip—”

“After you’ve rested.”

He turned away before either of them could make the next sentence worse.
