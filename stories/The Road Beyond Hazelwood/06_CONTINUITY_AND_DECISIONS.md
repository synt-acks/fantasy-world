## 2. Approved direction in practical terms

The manuscript is not being rewritten as an epic war or a Slayer recruitment story. It is a survival novel about Philip carrying his injured father through a dangerous route to Malcore. The Slayers, the wayknife, the giant, and the city’s road system all matter because they press on the same question: how do people protect one another without deciding for one another?

The father–son confrontation has two planned stages:

1. **Early confrontation:** Father gives Philip incomplete information about the knife and the trip. Philip says that being kept ignorant is not safety. They still need one another, so the argument remains unresolved.
2. **Climactic confrontation:** Father tries to send Philip away from the city’s route decision. Philip names the pattern: Father demands he fight when he is being bullied, then refuses to listen when Philip offers a different kind of strength. Father acknowledges his fear and trusts Philip’s evidence. This changes action before it changes feeling.

Magic should support that conflict. The wayknife comes from Father’s Slayer past, but its use is not the same as inheriting Father’s role. Philip learns to read a path carefully and then choose what to do with the evidence.

## 3. Planned magic decision

**PLANNED system:** Waykeeping, a limited craft of reading repeated travel traces from worked markers with a dwarven wayknife.

- The knife provides local directional and rhythm impressions, not identity, safety, distance, destination, or intention.
- A heavy or recent track can mask older traffic.
- The reading causes short-term imbalance and repeated use creates nausea and disorientation.
- No teleportation, mind control, magical combat bonus, or healing.
- Philip’s success depends on comparing the reading with the terrain, tracks, Filroy’s knowledge, and Father’s memory.

The wayknife is an ordinary dagger as well as a magical tool. It does not make Philip a chosen one, and he cannot defeat the giant with it. Filroy’s speech remains a separate wonder whose explanation is open and not plot-critical.

## 4. Planned family conflict

- Father’s love is real; so is the harm caused by his secrecy and commands.
- Father’s wife died in an orc raid while he was away. His grief and guilt are about absence and helplessness; the plot does not reveal that he caused her death.
- Father tells Philip to fight back because he fears Philip will be hurt. He hides the Slayer past because he fears it will shape Philip’s life. Those impulses conflict, and Philip is right to challenge them.
- Philip does not owe immediate forgiveness. The ending is a beginning: Father shares the truth and offers skills on Philip’s terms; Philip chooses what he wants to learn.
- Self-defense can be useful without making violence Philip’s identity or the novel’s only definition of strength.

## 5. Planned external conflict

- A giant’s mountain shelf collapses; it moves toward Stillstone Hollow along an old route.
- Its passage damages the forest and roads. It is dangerous when trapped or attacked, but it is not hunting humans or intentionally targeting Malcore.
- The city’s modern barriers obscure the old route’s eastward turn. An outdated marker reading looks like a direct approach to the gate.
- Aro favors killing the giant because of his past loss. Malcore’s officials need verifiable evidence. Philip combines old traces, current behavior, terrain, and help to establish a safer route.
- The city clears the route and evacuates the approach. The giant chooses the open path to the hollow; nobody controls or domesticates it.

## 6. Continuity constraints

1. **Geography:** Hazelwood is north/upstream; Malcore is south/downstream; Stillstone Hollow is east of Malcore. The southbound journey occupies Days 2–6 overall; the city crisis is on Day 6.
2. **Waykeeping:** Only worked markers can be read; a marker must remain sufficiently intact to respond. Damaged markers give partial or conflicting impressions. No reading guarantees safety or reveals intention.
3. **Injury:** Father has a concussion and a closed lower-leg fracture. He wakes gradually, speaks in intervals, cannot walk unaided, and needs ordinary treatment. Recovery takes weeks. Philip has a scalp cut, bruised hand, mild concussion, and accumulated fatigue; the story must not treat him as physically unaffected.
4. **Philip’s competence:** He has no Slayer training at the start. He can fail, tire, misread, and ask for help. Shop skills and arithmetic support his observations but do not replace experience. His head injury and the wayknife’s sensory cost are distinct sources of disorientation.
5. **Giant:** It survives. It stays dangerous, nonverbal, and wild. The city’s plan changes because its route is understood, not because the creature becomes harmless.
6. **Aro:** The Alcrog story is not destiny. His friend died after killing the beast. Aro’s grief motivates his caution; he chooses not to shoot after evidence and opportunity change.
7. **Filroy:** He knows the local forest and acts for his own reasons. He cannot solve city politics, routekeeping, or the giant’s behavior by himself.
8. **Mother:** Her death is part of the orc raids. No secret magical cause, concealed survival, or retroactive betrayal is planned.
9. **The storm:** Natural severe weather; lightning is not linked to the wayknife.
10. **Ending:** The immediate city danger, Father’s treatment, and Philip’s agency resolve. The old Slayers do not reform; a larger world threat is not introduced.

## 8. Change-impact rules

If the author changes a major decision, review these dependencies before revising the plan:

- **Change the magic system:** Recheck M-02/M-03, F-05/F-06/F-10, the route office scenes, Chapters 10–11 and 20, and the climax in Chapters 26–27.
- **Change the giant’s motive or destination:** Recheck the geography, Filroy’s role, Aro’s opposition, M-03, Chapters 13, 19, 24, 27, and the resolution.
- **Change Father’s secrecy or his Slayer history:** Recheck M-01/M-04, the father–son arc, Chapters 2–3, 11, 17, 23, 25, and 28.
- **Change the ending or kill the giant:** Reassess the story’s restraint theme, Aro’s arc, F-04/F-08, the author-approved A+B direction, and whether the new title still fits.
- **Change the title:** Update all six project documents and any future manuscript metadata; preserve the source title only in provenance notes.

## 9. Future manuscript continuity workflow

When prose drafting begins, completed chapters become the primary story canon. After each chapter, update:

- facts established and any departures from this plan;
- Philip’s and Father’s knowledge and emotional state;
- magic demonstrations, costs, and limitations;
- route, weather, injury, supply, and object locations;
- planted clue IDs and completed payoffs;
- future chapters affected by a major change.

Do not silently turn a planned event into an established fact or change an accepted author decision.
