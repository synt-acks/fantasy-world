# Editorial Notes

## Chapter 1 — “River Rope”

- This is the first voice sample for the novel; maintain close third person through Philip, with occasional warm narrator observations.
- The river scene treats the bullying and physical danger sincerely. Keep humor outside the harm itself.
- Philip’s competence is practical attention, not combat ability. He checks the rope’s anchor before trusting it.
- Father’s protective intent and controlling advice both remain visible. Keep the disagreement unresolved until shared danger changes how they communicate.
- Chapter 1 contains no active magic. Introduce Waykeeping only where the approved blueprint calls for it.

## Chapter 2 — “The Arrow Seller”

- Keep Aro’s Alcrog story affecting rather than triumphant: his friend’s courage did not erase the cost.
- The shared name is coincidence and thematic echo. Do not imply prophecy or kinship.
- Philip hears only fragments of Father and Aro’s private Elvish conversation. Preserve the mystery around the north-road warning.

## Chapter 3 — “The Early Trip”

- Father’s reason is partly true but incomplete. Keep Philip’s knowledge limited to the giant rumor and the changed travel plan.
- The locked box is a gift tied to Father’s Slayer past, not an assignment or prophecy. Do not open it before the crash sequence.
- The family leaves before dawn on Day 2. Carry Philip’s river bruises forward without treating them as resolved.

## Chapters 4–6 — “Shelf Road,” “Wreckage,” and “The Knife in the Box”

- The crash is a physical failure caused by eroded support and heavy earlier pressure, not magic or sabotage.
- Father’s leg injury and concussion persist. Philip is also injured and disoriented; keep his effort and judgment limited by those conditions.
- The wreck destroys most supplies. Do not make the fire or the horses a convenient source of food or transport.
- The first wayknife response is brief, faint, and local to the cracked worked fitting. Philip does not know the rules and does not follow it.
- The old road fitting, road damage, and wagon collapse are connected by physical causes; the knife does not cause or repair the collapse.

## Chapters 7–9 — “The Berry Tree,” “A Stretcher and a Choice,” and “The Fox at the Treeline”

- Philip’s food and water remain scarce. The berries are a small temporary supply, not a solution to the journey.
- The northward route is physically blocked; Philip chooses Malcore on his own assessment after Father names the healer.
- The stretcher requires ongoing physical effort and checks. Keep Philip’s head and hand injuries consequential.
- Filroy has local knowledge and his own motive; do not make him omniscient, obedient, or a wayknife interpreter.
- Filroy’s speech remains unexplained and separate from the road magic.

## Chapters 10–12 — “A Road with No Road,” “Half Awake,” and “The Swamp’s False Road”

- Wayknife readings stay local to worked markers and report traffic, not safe passage. Keep the misreading understandable and the physical cost brief.
- Father has only a short waking window. His injury and confusion limit what he can tell Philip; the fuller Slayer history remains for later.
- The father–son argument advances but does not resolve. Their agreement is practical and fragile.
- The swamp crossing depends on Philip testing ground and accepting Filroy’s local knowledge, not on magic.
- The clear-looking seep is a small resource, not a cure for their thirst or medical needs.

## Chapters 13–21 — “The Giant’s Wake” through “Aro’s Arrows”

- The giant’s first appearance should stay awe-filled and frightening. It is not a comic spectacle, and its damage does not prove hostile intent.
- Philip’s route choice in Chapter 14 is his own assessment. Father’s brief confirmation does not decide for him; Philip still wants home.
- Keep the storm natural and the bridge failure physical. The line holds only where its anchor holds; the wayknife’s echo adds disorientation and no useful location information.
- Chapter 16 uses Filroy’s local knowledge, Philip’s persistence, and a careful improvised splint. Father’s concussion and closed fracture continue to limit him.
- Chapter 17 discloses the mission and the history without excusing Father’s secrecy. His grief over Philip’s mother is about his absence, not culpability for the raid.
- Aro is fallible and grieving, not a villain. The Alcrog story should include fear and cost, and the shared name remains coincidence.
- Wayknife readings in Chapters 18–20 are limited, local, and potentially misleading. Philip compares multiple traces with visible ground, Filroy’s knowledge, and Father’s memory; he does not treat any trace as a command.
- The giant’s close pass establishes danger when startled, while its route choices and avoidance of the fox den support—but do not prove—the shelter theory.
- The old Slayer use of route diversion should remain morally mixed. Protection can become control when local people do not know what risks are being moved toward them.

## Chapters 22–28 — “Malcore’s Gate” through “The Hollow Beyond”

- The gate wicket opens through Philip’s mechanical observation, not magic. Keep the crowd and gate staff under real pressure without making them cruel or foolish.
- Malcore’s healer provides ordinary stabilization and rest. Father does not recover overnight; Philip’s injuries and exhaustion remain visible.
- The roadwarden needs evidence and protects both the city and outer farms. She is cautious, not corrupt or obstructive for its own sake.
- Hedd and the workers verify the road in stages. A cart crossing the drainage is useful evidence but cannot establish that a giant can cross it.
- Chapter 25 is the peak Philip–Father confrontation. Father’s fear explains his control without making the harm acceptable; Philip can remain angry after Father lets him keep the knife.
- The climax works through evacuation, route clearing, a single costly reading, Aro’s restraint, and the giant’s own choice. No one controls, tames, or defeats it.
- Chapter 28 resolves the immediate crisis and begins repair. Father’s apology does not erase the argument; Philip chooses road learning and self-defense while keeping the shop in his future.
